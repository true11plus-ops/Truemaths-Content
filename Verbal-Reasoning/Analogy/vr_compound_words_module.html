What is this question type?

You are given two sets of brackets, each with three words. Your job is to pick one word from the left bracket and one word from the right bracket to build a single, real English word — with no space or hyphen in the middle. The left word always comes first. For example: (sun, bath, play) (room, ground, shine) → the answer is bathroom (bath + room).

✓ 1 · Closed Compound Identification

🌟 Simple Explanation

Imagine sticking two Lego bricks together so tightly that they look like one brick. A compound word is like that — two regular words joined with no gap and no dash. Football = foot + ball. Bedroom = bed + room. If you need a space or a dash, it doesn't count for this question type!

📚 Full Explanation

A closed compound word is a single lexical unit formed by two free morphemes written together without a space or hyphen. The GL Assessment exclusively tests this form. Open compounds (ice cream) and hyphenated compounds (mother-in-law) are never the correct answer. The resulting word must appear in a standard British English dictionary as a single entry.

🔍 Worked Example 1 — Step by Step

Question:

(bed, fire, cup)
+
(room, side, board)
1
Take "bed" → try bed + room = bedroom ✓ (a real, single word)
2
Check: is it written without a space? Yes. Is it in the dictionary? Yes. ✓
3
Quick check others: cup + board = cupboard ✓ — but bed + room came first and is valid, so we keep that.
4
In a real test, only ONE pair will make a proper closed compound. The answer here is bed, room.
⚠️ Watch Out!

The most common mistake: choosing words that make a phrase instead of a single word. "Back yard" is two words — but backyard is one. Always ask: "Would I write this as one word in a sentence?" If not, skip it.

✓ 2 · Combinatorial Logic (The 1-to-3 Scan)

🌟 Simple Explanation

Think of it like trying all combinations of toppings on a pizza. You have 3 bases and 3 toppings — you try base 1 with topping 1, then topping 2, then topping 3. Then move to base 2. You don't skip any combination. That way, you never miss the answer hiding in the middle!

📚 Full Explanation

With 3 words in each bracket, there are 9 possible pairs to test. A systematic approach — pairing each left word with all three right words before moving on — ensures no combination is missed. Elite students internalize this as a mental grid and run through it in under 10 seconds.

sidestandman
underunderside ✗understand ✓underman ✗
outoutside ✗outstand ✗outman ✗
firefireside ✗firestand ✗fireman ✗

↑ Working through the grid systematically: only understand is a real closed compound.

🔍 Worked Example — Grid Method

Question: (hand, back, sun) (side, flower, work)

hand+side→✗ not a word
hand+flower→✗ not a word
hand+work→handwork ✓
back+side→✗
sun+flower→sunflower ✓

Both seem valid — but sunflower is more established as a single closed compound. In GL, the question design ensures uniqueness. Answer: sun, flower.

⚠️ Watch Out!

Students often stop scanning as soon as they find something that "sounds okay." Always verify uniqueness — quickly run through all 9 pairs before committing. The obvious-looking pair is sometimes a trap!

✓ 3 · Morpheme Anchors

🌟 Simple Explanation

Some word-parts are like magnets — they stick to lots of other words. -work is a magnet: it grabs onto hand, foot, frame, net, over. When you see a "magnet" word in a bracket, test it with ALL the words from the other bracket first. It's often the quickest shortcut!

📚 Full Explanation

Certain morphemes — the smallest meaningful units of language — function as highly productive "anchor" words in compound formation. When these appear in a bracket, they dramatically narrow your search. Common anchor endings: -work, -land, -side, -ward, -board, -house, -light, -time, -stone, -stand. Common anchor starters: over-, under-, back-, out-, hand-, head-. Identifying an anchor in seconds is a key differentiator for elite performance.

🔍 Worked Example 1

Question: (over, sun, sea) (time, board, stone)

1
Spot the anchors in the right bracket: time, board, stone — all common endings.
2
Test "over" with each: overtime ✓ / overboard ✓ / overstone ✗
3
"overtime" and "overboard" both work — but in a real GL question, only one will appear. Here "overtime" is the more common and established answer.

Answer: over + time

⚠️ Watch Out!

Don't always anchor-search in the right bracket. Sometimes the anchor is on the LEFT (like "over" or "under"). If you scan right-bracket anchors first and find nothing, flip your strategy: look for anchor starters in the left bracket.

✓ 4 · Semantic Shift

🌟 Simple Explanation

Sometimes two words join together and create a completely different meaning — like a magic trick! Under + stand = understand has nothing to do with physically standing under anything. Over + look = overlook doesn't mean "look over a wall." When you combine words, the new word might mean something totally unexpected. Don't be tricked — trust whether it's a real word, not whether the meaning makes sense from the parts.

📚 Full Explanation

Semantic shift occurs when a compound word acquires a meaning that is not directly predictable from its component parts. This is called lexicalization. Examples: understand (not "stand under"), overlook (not "look over something"), withdraw (not "draw with"). Students must accept the compound as a single vocabulary unit, ignoring the literal meaning of the parts.

🔍 Worked Example 1

Question: (under, out, fire) (stand, man, side)

1
"understand" — does it mean "standing under"? No. But it IS a real word. ✓
2
"outman" — is it a real word? Not commonly. ✗
3
"fireside" — real word, and the meaning DOES match the parts. ✓ But "understand" is the stronger, more classic GL answer here.

Answer: under + stand

⚠️ Watch Out!

Students reject correct answers because "they don't make sense." If you know it's a real English word, choose it — even if the meaning seems unrelated to the two parts. Understand, overlook, withdraw are classic examples. Don't overthink the meaning!

🔍 Worked Example 2 — Tricky Semantic Shift
(over, back, hand)
(look, yard, shake)

overlook = to miss/ignore something (not "look over"). This is a shifted meaning. backyard = garden at the back. handshake = greeting with hands. All three work! In a GL test question, only one pair would appear per question — the designer ensures uniqueness.

✓ 5 · Spelling Integrity

🌟 Simple Explanation

In this question type, neither word ever changes its spelling when you join them. It's like snapping two Lego bricks together — the bricks don't change shape. So "happy" + "ness" would not work here, because the 'y' would need to change to 'i' to make "happiness." If joining two words requires ANY spelling change — reject it immediately!

📚 Full Explanation

GL Assessment compound word questions exclusively use words whose spellings remain identical when joined. This rules out:
• Words requiring -y → -i changes (happy → happily)
• Words requiring doubled consonants (run → running)
• Words requiring dropped letters (argue → argument)
The spelling integrity rule acts as a fast elimination filter: if any spelling modification is needed, the pair is categorically wrong.

🔍 Worked Example 1

Question: (happy, over, back) (ness, board, yard)

1
"happy + ness" → to make "happiness," you must change y to i. ✗ REJECTED.
2
"over + board" → overboard. No spelling change. ✓
3
"back + yard" → backyard. No spelling change. ✓

If this were a real GL question with only one valid answer, the design would ensure only one works. The spelling rule instantly eliminated "happy+ness."

⚠️ Watch Out!

The most common mistake: choosing a pair that makes a word you know ("happiness") but forgetting that the spelling changed. GL never includes pairs that require spelling modification. Use this as a fast elimination rule — it saves precious seconds.

🔍 Worked Example 2

weigh + side → "wayside" — but this requires changing "weigh" to "way"! Spelling changes = REJECTED. ✗

Answer: rain + bow

✓ 6 · Common Traps

🌟 Simple Explanation

The question-setters know exactly where students go wrong and put those "tempting wrong answers" right in front of you. It's like a banana peel on the floor — they WANT you to slip. The three main banana peels are: (1) a pair that makes a phrase but not one word, (2) a pair where you mix up which bracket it came from, (3) a pair that sounds like a real word but needs different spelling.

📚 Full Explanation

GL Assessment compound questions contain precisely engineered distractors. The three primary trap types are: Phrasal Verb Trap (e.g., "pick up" is a phrase, not a compound — but "uphold" is); Reverse Order Trap (seeing "butterfly" but "butter" is in the left bracket where "fly" is not available); Phonetic Spelling Trap ("weigh + side" looks like "wayside" but the spelling would need to change). A fourth emerging trap: the Almost Compound — a pairing that sounds logical but isn't lexicalized.

🔍 Worked Example — The Reverse Trap
(fly, butter, house)
(fire, milk, boat)
1
You see "butter" and "fly" — and think "butterfly!" But wait: "butter" is in Bracket 1 and "fly" is ALSO in Bracket 1 — not Bracket 2.
2
You cannot combine two words from the same bracket. The order is: LEFT word first, RIGHT word second.
3
Test properly: fly + fire ✗, fly + milk ✗, fly + boat = flyboat (rare), butter + fire ✗, butter + milk = buttermilk ✓

Answer: butter + milk

⚠️ Watch Out!

Always check WHICH bracket each word comes from before getting excited about a pair. "Butterfly" is a real word, but if both "butter" and "fly" are in the same bracket, the question is trying to trick you. Left bracket → right bracket. Never left → left or right → right.

🔍 Worked Example 2 — The Phrasal Verb Trap
(pick, set, up)
(stairs, lift, hold)

Students often pick "pick + up" = "pickup" (plausible in casual use). But GL avoids slang compounds. "up + hold" = uphold — a proper closed compound where the left word is "up" and the right is "hold." That's the answer: up + hold.
