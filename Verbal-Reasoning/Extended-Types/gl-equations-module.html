One-Step Equations

✅
Sub-skill 1 of 5
🧒 Simple Explanation

Think of the equals sign like a set of weighing scales. Both sides must weigh the same. The missing number is what you need to add to one side to make it balance.

If I say "5 + ? = 12" — the scales have 12 on the right. The left side has 5 plus something mystery. What do I need to add to 5 to make 12? Count up: 5 + 7 = 12. The missing number is 7. Simple!

The trick: Do the opposite operation to find the missing number.

📘 Full Explanation — Inverse Operations

Each operation has an opposite that "undoes" it. To isolate the missing value, apply the inverse operation to both sides of the equation:

  • Addition ↔ Subtraction    |    If ? + 6 = 13, then ? = 13 − 6 = 7
  • Multiplication ↔ Division   |    If ? × 4 = 28, then ? = 28 ÷ 4 = 7

Always perform a check: substitute your answer back into the original equation to confirm both sides are equal.

✏️ Worked Example
20 − ( ) = 8
1
Identify: The missing number is being subtracted from 20 to give 8.
2
Do the opposite: subtraction → addition. Rearrange: ( ) = 20 − 8
3
Calculate: 20 − 8 = 12
4
Check: 20 − 12 = 8 ✓
✅ Answer: 12
⚠️ Watch Out!
Division reversal trap: For 50 ÷ ( ) = 10, many students write 50 ÷ 10 = 5 and think the answer is 5. Check: 50 ÷ 5 = 10 ✓. The answer IS 5 — but make sure you understand WHY. You need 50 ÷ something to equal 10, so that something = 50 ÷ 10.
Half-solved trap: Never stop after calculating one side. Always double-check by putting your answer back into the original equation.
✏️ Harder Example — Division
48 ÷ ( ) = 6
1
Read carefully: 48 divided by something equals 6.
2
Do the opposite: division → multiply by result. ( ) = 48 ÷ 6
3
Calculate: 48 ÷ 6 = 8
4
Check: 48 ÷ 8 = 6 ✓
✅ Answer: 8

Brackets First — BODMAS

✅
Sub-skill 2 of 5
🧒 Simple Explanation

Brackets are like a queue-jumper at a shop. Normally you go in order, but anything in brackets gets served first — no matter what.

If you see (3 + 4) × 2, the brackets jump the queue. First: 3 + 4 = 7. Then: 7 × 2 = 14. Never do 4 × 2 first just because it's on the right — the bracket always wins.

The rule: Brackets → Order → Divide → Multiply → Add → Subtract

📘 Full Explanation — BODMAS & Brackets

BODMAS defines the order in which operations must be performed. In GL Assessment equations, brackets are used to force a specific calculation order. When the missing number is inside the brackets, you must work backwards through the outer operations first.

  • For ( ) outside brackets: solve the bracket first, then find the missing value
  • For ( ) inside brackets: find what the bracket must equal, then work inside
✏️ Worked Example — Missing number outside bracket
3 × (2 + 4) = ( )
1
Brackets first: 2 + 4 = 6
2
Then multiply: 3 × 6 = 18
3
Check: 3 × (2 + 4) = 3 × 6 = 18 ✓
✅ Answer: 18
⚠️ Watch Out!
Operation blindness: The biggest mistake is reading left to right and multiplying before checking for brackets. Always look at the whole equation first — spot any brackets before you touch any numbers.
Missing-number-inside-bracket: For ([ ] + 6) × 2 = 20, don't start with ×2. Instead work backwards: if the whole thing = 20, then the bracket must equal 20 ÷ 2 = 10, so [ ] + 6 = 10, giving [ ] = 4.
✏️ Harder Example — Missing number inside bracket
(( ) + 6) × 2 = 20
1
Work backwards from the outside: the whole equation = 20. The ×2 is outside the bracket, so the bracket must equal: 20 ÷ 2 = 10
2
Now solve inside: ( ) + 6 = 10, so ( ) = 10 − 6 = 4
3
Check: (4 + 6) × 2 = 10 × 2 = 20 ✓
✅ Answer: 4

Both Sides Need Working Out

✅
Sub-skill 3 of 5
🧒 Simple Explanation

Sometimes both sides of the equation have calculations to do before you can find the missing number. The trick: solve the side you can solve fully first, then use that answer to find the missing number on the other side.

Think of it as two recipe cards. One recipe you can make right now because you have all the ingredients. Make it, find out how many portions it gives. Then figure out how many of ingredient X you need for the other recipe to make the same number of portions.
📘 Full Explanation — Two-Sided Equations

When both sides of the equation contain operations, follow this process:

  1. Identify which side is fully known (no missing number). Calculate it completely.
  2. Set up the other side as an equation: known value = expression with missing number.
  3. Apply inverse operations to find the missing number.
  4. Verify by substituting back into both sides.
✏️ Worked Example
6 × 4 + ( ) = 50 − 10
1
Solve the right side first (no missing number): 50 − 10 = 40
2
Simplify left side: 6 × 4 = 24, so left becomes 24 + ( ) = 40
3
Find missing number: ( ) = 40 − 24 = 16
4
Check: 6 × 4 + 16 = 24 + 16 = 40 and 50 − 10 = 40 ✓
✅ Answer: 16
⚠️ Watch Out!
Half-solved thinking: The most common error is only solving one side and forgetting to go back to the other. Always solve the fully-known side completely before touching the side with the missing number.
Order matters even on the known side: If the known side has brackets, use BODMAS there too. Don't just go left to right.
✏️ Harder Example — Scholarship Trap
(8 + 4) × 2 = 96 ÷ ( )
1
Left side — brackets first: (8 + 4) = 12, then 12 × 2 = 24
2
Set up right side: 96 ÷ ( ) = 24
3
Do the opposite: ( ) = 96 ÷ 24 = 4
4
Check: (8+4)×2 = 24 and 96 ÷ 4 = 24 ✓
✅ Answer: 4

The Shortcut: Test the Options

✅
Sub-skill 4 of 5
🧒 Simple Explanation

Sometimes the fastest way to solve a missing number question is to try the answer options and see which one works. Instead of working backwards, you work forwards — just plug in each option and check.

It's like trying keys in a lock. Instead of building the key from scratch, you try each one. As soon as the door opens, you're done. If you have 4 options, you might crack it on the first try!

When to use this: If the equation looks complicated OR if you have multiple choice options — try plugging them in first.

📘 Full Explanation — Substitution Strategy

Substitution means replacing the missing number with each option and testing if the equation balances. This is especially fast when:

  • The equation has division (harder to reverse mentally)
  • You have MCQ options — just test each one
  • You can spot the right answer is likely small (try 1, 2, 3, 4...)

GL exam tip: If an equation looks long and messy, test the smallest option first. GL tends to use answers between 1–20.

✏️ Worked Example — Substitution Table
24 = 6 × ( )

Rather than dividing, we test each option:

Try ( ) =6 × ( )= 24?

36 × 3 = 18✗ No

✅ Answer: 4 — found by testing, no division needed.
⚠️ Watch Out!
Answer hunting trap: Some students scan for the "biggest" or "most obvious" number rather than testing properly. Always check your answer actually works in the equation — don't just guess.
Don't always substitute: For simple one-step equations, direct solving is faster. Use substitution when reversing the operation mentally feels slow or risky.

Decimals and Halves

✅
Sub-skill 5 of 5
🧒 Simple Explanation

GL sometimes uses simple decimal numbers in equations. The most common ones are 0.5 (which is the same as half, or ½) and 1.5 (one and a half).

0.5 × 10 is just "half of 10" = 5. Whenever you see 0.5 multiplied by something, just halve that number. See 1.5? It's just "one whole plus a half" — so 1.5 × 4 = 4 + 2 = 6.

Key fact: 0.5 = ½ = half. Multiplying by 0.5 is the same as dividing by 2.

📘 Full Explanation — Decimals in GL Equations

GL Assessment equations involving decimals almost always use simple halves. Key rules:

  • × 0.5 = divide by 2   →   0.5 × 8 = 4
  • ÷ 0.5 = multiply by 2   →   8 ÷ 0.5 = 16
  • × 1.5 = multiply by 1, then add half   →   1.5 × 6 = 6 + 3 = 9
  • If the result should be a whole number, the missing decimal is likely 0.5 or 1.5
✏️ Worked Example
1.5 × 4 = ( )
1
Think of 1.5 as 1 + 0.5: 1 × 4 = 4 and 0.5 × 4 = 2
2
Add together: 4 + 2 = 6
3
Check: 1.5 × 4 = 6 ✓
✅ Answer: 6
⚠️ Watch Out!
Decimal confusion: 0.5 is NOT 5. Students sometimes multiply by 5 when they see 0.5. Remember: 0.5 is half of 1 — it makes numbers smaller when you multiply, not bigger.
Result check: If your missing number turns out to be a decimal like 3.7 or 8.3, you've likely made an error — GL sticks to simple halves (0.5, 1.5, 2.5). Recheck your working.
✏️ Harder Example — Missing decimal
10 × ( ) = 5
1
Something × 10 = 5 — the result is smaller than what you're multiplying by. The missing number must be less than 1.
2
Do the opposite: ( ) = 5 ÷ 10 = 0.5
3
Check: 10 × 0.5 = 5 ✓
✅ Answer: 0.5 (which is the same as ½)

Master Strategy — 30 Seconds Per Question

⚡ The Decision Tree — Which Method to Use?
  1. Quick look (2 sec): Is there one obvious step? → Solve directly. e.g. ( ) + 6 = 15 → direct subtraction.
  2. Brackets present? → Solve brackets first. If missing number is inside bracket, work backwards from outside in.
  3. Both sides have operations? → Solve the fully-known side completely first. Then apply inverse to the remaining side.
  4. MCQ options available? → Consider substitution. Plug in each option, smallest first. Stop when it works.
  5. Always check: Substitute your answer back into the original equation. Both sides must be equal.
🧠 Common Traps — Quick Reference
❌ Common Errors
Ignoring brackets → wrong order
50 ÷ ( ) = 10, answer is NOT 10
Stopping after one side only
Going left-to-right, skipping BODMAS
0.5 × n = n × 5 ← wrong!
✅ Good Habits
Brackets always first
Solve fully-known side first
"Do the opposite" to isolate
Always substitute and check
0.5 = half → divides by 2
