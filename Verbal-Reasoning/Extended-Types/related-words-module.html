Related Words

Master the hidden connection between word groups — and never fall for a trap answer again.

📋

How This Question Works

Simple Explanation

You see three groups of words. The first two groups give you a clue — they connect in the same way. Your job: find the word that finishes the third group following the exact same rule. Think of it like cracking a secret code!

The GL Format

Layout:

(apple, pear, orange)   (carrot, potato, onion)   (beef, pork, ______)

You get 5 options (A–E) and roughly 30–40 seconds. Speed matters — but accuracy matters more.

📚 Reference: Parts of Speech

🏃 Doing words = Verbs🏷️ Naming words = Nouns🎨 Describing words = Adjectives➡️ How words = Adverbs

1. Same Kind of Thing

✅
Simple Explanation

Words in each group all belong to the same category. But be careful — the category might be very specific. It's not just "animals" — it might be "pets you keep at home." Always check your rule is specific enough by testing it on BOTH groups before moving to the third.

Full Explanation

Semantic categorization means grouping words by shared meaning or real-world class. The key trap is near-miss answers — words belonging to a related but broader or narrower category. Your rule must fit ALL words in groups 1 AND 2 before you apply it.

🔍 Worked Example (Standard)

(oak, pine, elm)   (rose, tulip, daisy)   (salmon, trout, ___)
  • 1Group 1: oak/pine/elm → all trees
  • 2Group 2: rose/tulip/daisy → all flowers. Rule: each group = one specific type of plant.
  • 3Group 3: salmon/trout → both fish. Need a specific type of fish.
  • 4Answer must be a fish — not any water creature.
✓ Answer: cod — a type of fish, matching the exact sub-category
⚠️ Watch Out!

The trap always feels right. If the category is "citrus fruits," the trap is "strawberry" (a fruit — but not citrus!). Always ask: is my rule specific enough?

🔍 Harder Example — Parts of a Whole

(leaf, branch, root)   (engine, wheel, door)   (chapter, page, ___)
  • 1Group 1: leaf/branch/root → parts of a tree
  • 2Group 2: engine/wheel/door → parts of a car. Rule: each group = physical parts of a complete whole object.
  • 3Group 3: chapter/page → parts of a book. Need another physical part of a book.
  • 4Trap: "Book" = the whole object, not a part. "Story" = abstract content. "Cover" ✓ = physical structural part.
✓ Answer: cover — a physical structural part of a book, like a leaf is part of a tree

2. Same Type of Word

✅
Simple Explanation

Sometimes the link is about what kind of word it is — not just what it means. All words might be doing words (verbs) or describing words (adjectives). Even the right meaning in the wrong form is wrong! Check: is it the same type AND the same form (ending)?

Full Explanation

Grammatical consistency means all words share the same part of speech AND the same form (e.g., all past tense, all -ing form, all plurals). A classic GL trap provides the correct meaning but the wrong form — e.g., giving run when all words are in the -ing form.

🔍 Worked Example

(shout, yell, scream)   (whisper, mumble, mutter)   (talk, speak, ___)
  • 1Group 1: shout/yell/scream → verbs for loud vocal sounds
  • 2Group 2: whisper/mumble/mutter → verbs for quiet/unclear speaking. All base-form verbs of vocal communication.
  • 3Check options: "Voice" = noun ✗, "Loudly" = adverb ✗, "Chat" = verb, base form ✓
✓ Answer: chat — a base-form verb of vocal communication
⚠️ Watch Out!

Always check the FORM, not just the meaning. "Ran" and "run" mean the same — but if the group uses base forms, "ran" is wrong. Past tense and base form are different!

🔍 Harder Example — Past Participles as Adjectives

(heated, frozen, grilled)   (tattered, polished, broken)   (written, spoken, ___)
  • 1Group 1: heated/frozen/grilled → past participles used as food descriptors
  • 2Group 2: tattered/polished/broken → past participles describing condition/state
  • 3Group 3: written/spoken → past participles describing communication type. Need another in the -en/-ed form.
  • 4"Printed" ✓ = past participle. "Print" ✗ = base form. "Printing" ✗ = -ing form.
✓ Answer: printed — a past participle used as an adjective, matching the grammatical form

3. More or Less — Scales & Degrees

✅
Simple Explanation

Some groups go from less to more — like a temperature dial. Cool → cold → freezing is a scale getting colder. Find which direction the scale goes, then pick the word that fits at the right position — usually the most extreme end!

Full Explanation

Intensity and degree questions test whether you recognise ordinal scales of meaning. Words share a dimension (temperature, size, emotion, speed) but differ in degree. The position within the group matters — a word must fit on the same scale AND at the same position (usually the extreme end).

🔍 Worked Example

(cool, cold, freezing)   (warm, hot, boiling)   (damp, wet, ___)
  • 1Group 1: cool/cold/freezing → increasing scale of cold
  • 2Group 2: warm/hot/boiling → increasing scale of heat. Pattern: mild → moderate → extreme.
  • 3Group 3: damp/wet/___ → increasing scale of wetness. Damp = a little wet. Wet = moderately wet. Need: extreme wetness.
  • 4"Drenched" or "soaked" = completely saturated — the extreme end of the wetness scale. ✓
✓ Answer: drenched — the extreme on the wetness scale, matching "freezing" and "boiling"
⚠️ Watch Out!

A word might be on the right scale but the wrong position. If the group goes mild → extreme, don't choose a middle word — find the extreme! Also check you're going in the right direction (stronger, not weaker).

🔍 Harder Example — Emotion Scales

(dislike, hate, detest)   (like, enjoy, love)   (surprised, astonished, ___)
  • 1Group 1: dislike→hate→detest: increasing negative feeling
  • 2Group 2: like→enjoy→love: increasing positive feeling. Both go mild → intense.
  • 3Group 3: surprised→astonished→___. "Astonished" is stronger than "surprised." Need even more extreme shock.
  • 4"Flabbergasted" = completely overwhelmed by surprise — the most extreme end. ✓
✓ Answer: flabbergasted — the most extreme degree of surprise on the scale

4. What Things Do — Functional Links

✅
Simple Explanation

Sometimes the link isn't what type of thing it is — it's what it does. A knife, scissors, and axe are all different objects, but they all cut things! Look for the shared job or purpose, not the shared appearance or category.

Full Explanation

Functional relationships group words by their shared action, purpose, or role — not by what they look like or what category they belong to. A pen and a keyboard both write. A telescope and glasses both help you see. The key question is: "What job does this word do?" Watch out for cross-domain pairs: doctor→heal, pen→write, needle→sew — the function links words from different worlds.

🔍 Worked Example

(knife, scissors, axe)   (file, sandpaper, grinder)   (drill, chisel, ___)
  • 1Group 1: knife/scissors/axe → tools that CUT
  • 2Group 2: file/sandpaper/grinder → tools that SMOOTH or ABRADE. Different function — refine your rule: "tools defined by their specific action on material."
  • 3Group 3: drill/chisel → tools that PIERCE or CHIP. What else does the same job? "Hammer" chips/strikes. ✓
  • 4Eliminate "wood" (material, not a tool) and "carpenter" (person, not a tool).
✓ Answer: hammer — a tool used to strike/chip, matching the "action tool" function pattern
⚠️ Watch Out!

Don't choose by category! "Fork" and "knife" are both cutlery — but "fork" doesn't cut. Focus on the exact function, not just the general world the word lives in. Also beware of cross-domain traps: the answer may come from a completely different field.

🔍 Harder Example — Cross-Domain Analogy

(doctor, nurse, surgeon)   (teacher, tutor, lecturer)   (pilot, captain, ___)
  • 1Group 1: doctor/nurse/surgeon → professionals who HEAL/TREAT people
  • 2Group 2: teacher/tutor/lecturer → professionals who EDUCATE/INSTRUCT
  • 3Group 3: pilot/captain → professionals who NAVIGATE/COMMAND a vehicle. Need another navigation professional.
  • 4"Driver" = steers a vehicle ✓ but is it at the same professional level? "Navigator" = directs course ✓. "Skipper" = commands a boat ✓.
✓ Answer: navigator — a professional who directs/steers a vehicle or vessel, matching the command/navigation function

Starter

5. Hidden Meanings — Homographs

✅
Simple Explanation

Some words have two completely different meanings! The word BARK can be the rough skin of a tree — OR the sound a dog makes. When a group uses a tricky word like this, your first meaning might be WRONG. Always ask: "Could this word mean something else?"

Full Explanation

Homographs are words spelled the same but carrying different meanings. GL Assessment deliberately uses these to misdirect you. The groups are built around a word's SECONDARY or less obvious meaning. For example: (Scale, Note, Key) — your brain says "weight/music/key ring," but the link is actually "things in music." Always test your first interpretation against Group 2 before committing.

🔍 Worked Example

(pound, ounce, gram)   (inch, mile, yard)   (second, hour, ___)
  • 1Group 1: pound/ounce/gram → units of WEIGHT
  • 2Group 2: inch/mile/yard → units of DISTANCE/LENGTH. Pattern refined: "standard units of measurement."
  • 3Group 3: second/hour → units of TIME measurement. Need another unit of time.
  • 4"Minute" = unit of time ✓. "Clock" = device not unit ✗. "Time" = the concept not a unit ✗.
✓ Answer: minute — a unit of time (its primary meaning here, though it also means "tiny"!)
⚠️ Watch Out!

Your first instinct is usually the TRAP. When you see "bark," you think dog. When you see "scale," you think weight. But the question is built to use the OTHER meaning. Always check your rule against BOTH groups before you're sure.

🔍 Harder Example — Music Pivot

(scale, note, rest)   (beat, bar, tempo)   (pitch, key, ___)
  • 1Group 1: scale/note/rest — your brain says "weight/paper/relaxing," but check Group 2.
  • 2Group 2: beat/bar/tempo → clearly MUSIC terms. So Group 1 is also music: scale (series of notes), note (musical sound), rest (silence in music).
  • 3Group 3: pitch/key → both music terms. "Pitch" = frequency of sound. "Key" = musical key. Need another music term.
  • 4"Chord" = multiple notes together ✓. "Sharp" = half-step up in music ✓.
✓ Answer: chord — a music term, using the non-obvious meaning to match the hidden pattern

6. Two Rules at Once — Dual Classification

✅
Simple Explanation

Some questions have TWO rules you must follow at the same time! Like finding a word that is BOTH a fruit AND red. An orange is a fruit — but it's not red. A strawberry is red AND a fruit — that's the answer! You must tick BOTH boxes, not just one.

Full Explanation

Dual-rule classification requires satisfying two simultaneous conditions. The groups establish TWO independent rules — category AND a qualifier (colour, size, function, grammar). An answer that satisfies only one rule is a deliberate trap. You must test EVERY option against BOTH conditions before selecting.

🔍 Worked Example

(crimson, scarlet, ruby)   (cherry, strawberry, tomato)   (rose, poppy, ___)
  • 1Group 1: crimson/scarlet/ruby → shades of RED
  • 2Group 2: cherry/strawberry/tomato → RED-coloured foods/fruits. Both groups are about red things. But Group 2 uses real red objects.
  • 3Group 3: rose/poppy → both RED flowers! Two rules: must be a FLOWER AND must be RED.
  • 4Test options: "tulip" — flower ✓, can be red ✓. "Daisy" — flower ✓, but white/yellow ✗. "Geranium" — flower ✓, can be red ✓.
✓ Answer: geranium — a flower that is characteristically red, satisfying BOTH the "flower" AND "red" conditions
⚠️ Watch Out!

The trap is always an option that satisfies ONE rule only. "Tulip" is a flower but comes in many colours. "Apple" is red but not a flower. Always check BOTH conditions before choosing — if you only check one, you'll fall into the trap.

🔍 Harder Example — Category + Grammar

(jogged, sprinted, dashed)   (crawled, slithered, crept)   (glided, floated, ___)
  • 1Rule 1 (grammar): all words are past tense verbs (-ed form)
  • 2Rule 2 (category): jogged/sprinted/dashed = fast land movement; crawled/slithered/crept = slow low movement; glided/floated = smooth effortless movement.
  • 3Must be: past tense AND a verb of smooth/effortless movement.
  • 4"Drift" ✗ = base form. "Drifted" ✓ = past tense + smooth movement. "Soared" ✓ = past tense + effortless movement.
✓ Answer: drifted — past tense verb (Rule 1) AND a smooth, effortless movement (Rule 2)

7. Ideas & Feelings — Abstract Categories

✅
Simple Explanation

Not all categories are things you can touch! Joy, fear, and sadness are all feelings — you can't hold them, but they belong in the same group. Honesty, courage, and kindness are all personal qualities. When you can't see or touch the words, ask: "Are these all feelings? Qualities? States of matter?"

Full Explanation

Abstract categories group words by shared invisible concepts: emotions (anger, joy, fear), personal virtues (honesty, bravery, loyalty), mental states (confusion, focus, clarity), or physical states (solid, liquid, gas). These are harder because students try to find a concrete category and fail. The correct approach: ask "What do these words describe that you cannot see or touch?"

🔍 Worked Example

(anger, joy, fear)   (honesty, bravery, kindness)   (confusion, focus, ___)
  • 1Group 1: anger/joy/fear → emotions (feelings)
  • 2Group 2: honesty/bravery/kindness → virtues (positive character traits). Different abstract category.
  • 3Group 3: confusion/focus → mental states (states of mind). Need another mental state.
  • 4"Clarity" = a state of clear thinking ✓. "Think" = an action, not a state ✗. "Brain" = concrete object ✗.
✓ Answer: clarity — an abstract mental state, matching confusion and focus exactly
⚠️ Watch Out!

The trap is always a concrete word from the same "world." If the group is about emotions, the trap might be "heart" (related to feelings but a concrete body part) or "cry" (what you do when sad, but an action not a feeling). The answer must be the same TYPE of abstract word — not just related to the theme.

🔍 Harder Example — States of Matter & Beyond

(solid, liquid, gas)   (peace, chaos, order)   (growth, decay, ___)
  • 1Group 1: solid/liquid/gas → states of matter (physical states)
  • 2Group 2: peace/chaos/order → states of society/organisation (abstract social states)
  • 3Group 3: growth/decay → states of change or progress. Need another abstract state of change.
  • 4"Stagnation" = a state of not changing (opposite of growth/decay) ✓. "Grow" = verb not noun ✗. "Plant" = concrete ✗.
✓ Answer: stagnation — an abstract state of change (or no change), matching growth and decay
