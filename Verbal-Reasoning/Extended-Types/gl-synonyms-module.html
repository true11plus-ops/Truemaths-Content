The Question Format

You are given two groups of three words. You must find one word from the first group and one word from the second group that are closest in meaning to each other.

GROUP 1                GROUP 2 ( large   cold   quick )    ( slow   fast   chilly )
Answer: quick — fast

Your answer must be the pair — one word from each bracket. "Quick" and "fast" mean the same thing. The other words are distractors.

Core Synonym Matching

✅
Sub-skill 1 of 5
🧒 Simple Explanation — Read This First

A synonym is just a word that means the same thing as another word. It's like different names for the same idea.

"Happy" and "joyful" are synonyms — they both describe the same feeling. Like how you might call your friend by their name or their nickname — two names, one person.

Your job: look at all six words across both brackets, and find the pair that are two words for the same thing.

📘 Full Explanation

In GL Assessment Verbal Reasoning, "Closest in Meaning" questions test your ability to identify synonymous pairs — words that share identical or near-identical semantic content. You must select exactly one word from Group 1 and one word from Group 2. The pair does not need to be perfect synonyms in every context — only the closest pairing from the available options. The question tests breadth of vocabulary, not just common words.

✏️ Worked Example — Step by Step
( begin   cold   heavy )    ( start   tall   dark )
1
Quick scan: Do any pairs jump out instantly? "begin" and "start" — that's a match you might know immediately!
2
Anchor on Group 1, word 1: "begin" — what does it mean? To start something. Is "start," "tall," or "dark" a synonym? → "start" ✔
3
Check the others to be sure: "cold" ≠ "start," "tall," "dark." "heavy" ≠ "start," "tall," "dark." No other pair works.
✅ Answer: begin — start (both mean 'to commence or initiate something')
⚠️ Watch Out!
Don't just pick the first pair you see — always verify by checking if any other pair could also work. If you're unsure, test all three words from Group 1 against all three in Group 2.
Don't confuse a word that is related to another with a true synonym. "Ice" and "cold" are related, but they don't mean the same thing.
✏️ Harder Worked Example
( miserable   loud   ancient )    ( old   cheerful   quiet )
1
Quick scan: Nothing jumps out instantly — be careful. Let's anchor methodically.
2
Test "miserable": Means very unhappy. "old"? No. "cheerful"? No — that's an antonym! "quiet"? No.
3
Test "loud": Means making a lot of noise. "old"? No. "cheerful"? No. "quiet"? No — opposite!
4
Test "ancient": Means very old. "old"? ✔ Yes! "Ancient" and "old" both mean something that has existed for a long time.
✅ Answer: ancient — old (both mean 'having existed for a very long time')

Degree & Intensity Traps

✅
Sub-skill 2 of 5
🧒 Simple Explanation

Some words mean almost the same thing but at different levels of strength. Imagine a temperature scale: cold → cool → warm → hot → boiling. They're all about temperature, but they're not the same!

It's like the difference between "big" and "enormous." Both mean large — but enormous is much, much bigger. The GL exam loves to put "big" in one group and "enormous" in another, and then also include "gigantic" to confuse you. You must find the closest match in strength.
📘 Full Explanation — Intensity Gradients

GL Assessment often uses degree precision traps: words that exist on the same intensity scale, but are not true synonyms of each other. A correct synonym pair must match in both meaning and approximate intensity. For example:

  • Warm ≠ Scalding — both describe heat, but at very different levels
  • Annoyed ≠ Furious — both describe anger, but furious is far stronger
  • Chilly ≠ Freezing — both describe cold, but freezing is far more intense

The correct pair must be the two words that are closest to each other on the intensity scale.

✏️ Worked Example — Degree Trap
( warm   freezing   boiling )    ( scalding   cool   chilly )
1
Map the intensity scale: freezing → chilly → cool → warm → boiling → scalding. All are temperature words.
2
Find the closest pair across both groups:
• boiling (Group 1) ↔ scalding (Group 2) — both mean extremely hot. ✔
• freezing (Group 1) ↔ chilly (Group 2) — freezing is much colder than chilly. ✗
• warm (Group 1) ↔ cool (Group 2) — these are opposites on the scale. ✗
✅ Answer: boiling — scalding (both mean dangerously, extremely hot)
⚠️ Watch Out!
Don't just pick any two words from the same theme (e.g., both about temperature). You must pick the pair that are closest to each other in degree.
"Freezing" and "chilly" are both cold — but they are NOT synonyms. Freezing is extreme; chilly is mild. The pair "boiling — scalding" are both extreme heat, making them the closer match.
✏️ Harder Example — Emotion Intensity
( cross   enraged   displeased )    ( furious   annoyed   content )
1
Build the anger scale: displeased → annoyed → cross → enraged → furious
2
Match closest pairs:
• enraged ↔ furious — both mean extremely angry ✔
• cross ↔ annoyed — both mean mildly to moderately angry ✔
• displeased ↔ annoyed — close but displeased is milder
3
Best single pair: "enraged — furious" is the most precise match (both extreme anger).
✅ Answer: enraged — furious (both mean extremely, intensely angry)

The Double-Match Trap

✅
Sub-skill 3 of 5
🧒 Simple Explanation

Sometimes it looks like there are two correct pairs — both seem to work! This is a trick. There is always only one best pair. You have to find the pair that match most exactly.

Imagine you have two keys that both fit the lock a little, but only one opens the door perfectly. The GL exam gives you two pairs that both seem like synonyms — but one pair matches more exactly than the other. You need the perfect key.
📘 Full Explanation — Resolving Double-Match Conflicts

In a Double-Match Trap, two pairs both appear to be synonyms. To resolve this, ask yourself: which pair has the most precise, complete overlap in meaning?

Example: (Bright, Smart, Quick) vs (Fast, Intelligent, Shiny)

  • Smart — Intelligent ✔ (both mean mentally capable — strong match)
  • Bright — Shiny ✔ (both can mean producing light — also a match)

Here, Smart — Intelligent is the more precise pair, because "Bright" has many meanings (clever, luminous) while "Shiny" only refers to reflected light. Choose the pair that is most unambiguous.

✏️ Worked Example — Double Match
( shut   close   quiet )    ( near   silent   seal )
1
Test all pairs:
• shut ↔ seal: both can mean to close something. ✔
• close ↔ near: both can mean nearby. ✔
• quiet ↔ silent: both mean making little or no noise. ✔
2
Which is most precise?
• "quiet — silent" — quiet means making very little noise; silent means making NO noise. Close, but not exact.
• "close — near" — both mean at a short distance. Very strong match.
• "shut — seal" — shut means closed; seal means to close permanently. Not quite equal.
3
Best answer: "close — near" — both mean at a small distance. Most precise and complete overlap.
✅ Answer: close — near (both mean at a short distance, equally strong match)
⚠️ Watch Out!
When two pairs both seem to work, don't just pick the first one you noticed. Always check all possible pairs, then pick the one with the most exact match in meaning.
Remember: words with multiple meanings (like "bright" meaning smart OR shiny) are often used as distractors. The correct answer is usually the pair whose meanings overlap most clearly and completely.
Developing
Two pairs might seem correct. Find the most precise synonym pair.
( smart   bright   quick )    ( fast   intelligent   shiny )
Secure
Be careful — more than one pair might seem close. Choose the most precise match.
( finish   end   stop )    ( cease   halt   conclude )
Mastery
All three pairs might look valid. Find the single most precise synonym pair.
( scared   angry   sad )    ( unhappy   frightened   cross )
Mastery
Type the most precise synonym pair from these words.
( cry   shout   whisper )    ( yell   sob   murmur )
💡 Hint: All three pairs might seem valid. Which pair is the most equally matched in meaning and intensity?

Unknown Word Strategy

✅
Sub-skill 4 of 5
🧒 Simple Explanation

Sometimes you'll see a word you don't know. Don't panic! You don't need to know every word to get the right answer.

Imagine a puzzle where 5 pieces fit perfectly and 1 piece is a mystery shape. You can still solve the puzzle by placing all the pieces you do know first — and the mystery piece goes in the only gap left. Same with words: use what you know to eliminate wrong pairs, and the unknown word's match reveals itself.
📘 Unknown Word Fallback Protocol

Follow this order:

  • If 1 word is unknown: test all pairs using the 5 known words first. Eliminate any pair where you know the words don't match. The unknown word may be left as the only remaining candidate.
  • If 2 words are unknown: eliminate pairs where you are certain the words are NOT synonyms. Narrow to the most likely remaining pair.
  • Use word roots: "bene-" means good, "mal-" means bad, "-ology" means study of. Even partial knowledge helps.
  • Speed rule: If 3+ words are unknown, make your best guess quickly and move on. Don't spend more than 30 seconds on any single question.
✏️ Worked Example — Unknown Word Elimination
( benevolent   cold   quick )    ( fast   hostile   kind )
1
"Benevolent" is unfamiliar. Set it aside. Test the words you know.
• cold ↔ fast? No. cold ↔ hostile? Possible (cold can mean unfriendly). cold ↔ kind? No (opposite!).
• quick ↔ fast? Yes — strong match! ✔
2
Verify "quick — fast" is clearly correct. Both mean rapid movement.
3
Check "cold — hostile": "cold" can mean unfriendly, and "hostile" means unfriendly/aggressive. Possible — but "quick — fast" is much more precise.
✅ Answer: quick — fast (both clearly mean moving rapidly — no ambiguity)
⚠️ Watch Out!
Don't freeze when you see an unknown word. Immediately switch to testing the pairs you DO know. Often the answer doesn't involve the unknown word at all.
Never guess randomly. Eliminate impossible pairs first, then make an informed guess from the remaining options.
✏️ Harder Example — Using Word Roots
( malicious   calm   happy )    ( peaceful   joyful   spiteful )
1
Known pairs: calm ↔ peaceful ✔ (strong match), happy ↔ joyful ✔ (strong match)
2
"Malicious" unfamiliar? The root "mal-" means bad/evil. "Spiteful" means deliberately hurtful. Malicious likely means intending harm. malicious ↔ spiteful — possible.
3
Three valid pairs! This is a double-match situation too. "happy — joyful" is the most exact and common synonym pair. In a GL exam, this would be the expected answer.
✅ Answer: happy — joyful (most precise and unambiguous synonym pair)

( arid   cold   bright )    ( luminous   freezing   dry )

Use elimination — the unfamiliar word's pair will become clear.
( loquacious   small   happy )    ( talkative   joyful   large )

Secondary Meaning & Homograph Traps

✅
Sub-skill 5 of 5
🧒 Simple Explanation

Some words have more than one meaning. The word "light" can mean a lamp, the colour white, or not heavy. The GL exam uses words like this to trick you into thinking about the wrong meaning.

It's like the word "bat" — it can mean the animal OR the thing you hit a cricket ball with. If someone asks "find the synonym of bat" and gives you options including "mammal" and "club," you need to figure out which meaning of "bat" they're testing!
📘 Homograph & Secondary Meaning Strategy

When a word could have multiple meanings, test every possible meaning of that word against the other group. Common traps include:

  • Charge: to rush at someone / to accuse / an electrical charge / a price
  • Fine: a penalty payment / excellent quality / very thin
  • Light: not heavy / pale in colour / a source of illumination
  • Grave: a burial place / serious / deeply concerned
  • Cross: angry / a shape / to move across
  • Bark: the outer covering of a tree / the sound a dog makes

Key rule: Don't assume the most common meaning is correct. Always try the secondary meanings too.

✏️ Worked Example — Secondary Meaning
( charge   fine   light )    ( fee   thin   accuse )
1
Test "charge" — it has multiple meanings:
• charge = rush at someone → fee? No. thin? No. accuse? No.
• charge = accuse (formally) → accuse! ✔
2
Test "fine":
• fine = penalty payment → fee? Yes! ✔
• fine = excellent → fee? No. thin? No. accuse? No.
• fine = slender/thin → thin? Yes! ✔
(Two possible pairs! Need to check further.)
3
Resolve the conflict: "fine — fee" (fine as a penalty) vs "fine — thin" (fine as slender). Fine means a slender quality in rare contexts, but "fee" is the more standard synonym for a financial fine. However, "charge — accuse" is the most precise and unambiguous pair.
✅ Answer: charge — accuse (both mean to formally blame someone for a wrongdoing)
⚠️ Watch Out!
The most common mistake is picking a word's everyday meaning without considering its secondary meanings. Always ask: "Does this word have another meaning I haven't thought of?"
Watch out for function/synonym confusion: "knife" and "cut" are related (a knife cuts), but they are NOT synonyms. Synonyms must mean the same thing, not just be associated with each other.
Part-of-speech traps: under pressure, students forget grammar. "Run" (verb: to move fast) is NOT a synonym of "run" (noun: a score in cricket). Make sure both words are being used as the same part of speech.

( cross   plain   cool )    ( chilly   angry   simple )

★

Master Strategy — 30 Seconds Per Question

⚡ The 5-Step Speed Strategy
  1. Quick scan (3 sec): Glance at all six words. Does any pair jump out as an obvious synonym? If yes, note it — but don't answer yet.
  2. Concrete → Abstract (10 sec): Start with the most straightforward, concrete words first. Test familiar, common-use words before unusual or abstract ones. Concrete matches are usually unambiguous.
  3. Anchor method (10 sec): Take word 1 from Group 1. Ask: does it mean the same as word 1, 2, or 3 from Group 2? If a match is found, note it. Repeat for words 2 and 3 from Group 1.
  4. Resolve conflicts (5 sec): If two pairs seem valid, ask which pair is more precise and unambiguous. Prefer pairs where both words are used in exactly the same way in identical contexts.
  5. Commit and move on (2 sec): Pick the best pair. Don't second-guess excessively. Mark it and proceed. A confident wrong answer is better than a blank.
