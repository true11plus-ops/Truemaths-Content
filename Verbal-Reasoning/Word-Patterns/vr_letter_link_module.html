1. The Core Mechanic — One Letter, Two Jobs

✓
🧒 Simple Explanation
Imagine a jigsaw piece that must fit into two different puzzles at the same time. In Letter Link questions, one single letter must complete the end of one word AND the start of another — and this must work for BOTH word pairs. Find the letter that does two jobs at once!
📖 Full Explanation
In GL Assessment Letter Link questions, you are given two word pairs in brackets. A single letter sits between the letters of each pair — it ends the first word and begins the second. The same letter must make valid words in both pairs simultaneously. Your task: find the one letter that satisfies both pairs.

2. The Anchor Word Strategy

✓
🧒 Simple Explanation
One pair almost always makes a more obvious word than the other. That obvious word is your anchor — like a signpost pointing straight to the answer letter. Find the pair that gives you the most obvious word first, get the letter from that, then check it works in the other pair. Don't waste time on the harder pair first!
📖 Full Explanation
The anchor word strategy identifies which of the two pairs is more lexically transparent — meaning one pair tends to suggest a familiar word more immediately. By identifying this pair and extracting the most likely link letter from it, you convert the problem from a two-pair scan into a single verification step. This halves your cognitive load under time pressure.
✏️ Worked Example — Spotting the Anchor
(BOA _ ING) (SPOR _ IVE)
1
Scan both pairs quickly: BOA_ING — could be BOATING, BOARING, BOASING? SPOR_IVE — SPORTIVE jumps out. SPORT is very familiar!
2
Anchor = Pair 2. SPOR+T = SPORT ✅. Link letter candidate: T.
3
Verify T in Pair 1: BOA+T = BOAT ✅ and T+ING = TING → BOATING ✅. Both pairs confirmed!
✅ Anchor word: SPORT (Pair 2) → T → verified in BOATING (Pair 1)
⏱️ Speed Rule — 10 Seconds
If you haven't spotted an anchor word within 10 seconds, switch immediately to the Options method: try each letter option in Pair 2 first (or whichever pair looks simpler), then verify in Pair 1. Never just mentally run through the whole alphabet — that's too slow.
⚠️ Watch Out!
Don't assume Pair 1 is always easier — the anchor could be in either pair! GL Assessment sometimes puts the harder pair first deliberately. Always glance at both before deciding where to start. The pair with the more obvious word is your anchor, regardless of which bracket it's in.
✏️ Harder Example — Anchor in Pair 2
(OUG _ LY) (STA _ LED)
1
Pair 1: OUG_LY — OUGHLY? This doesn't suggest an obvious word quickly.
2
Pair 2: STA_LED — STARTLED? STA+R+TLED = STARTLED? Or STABLED: STA+B+LED = STABLED ✅. Or STAPLED: STA+P+LED = STAPLED ✅. STABLED is the clearest anchor.
3
Try B (from STABLED): OUG+B+LY = OUGBLY ✗. Fail. Try P (from STAPLED): OUG+P+LY = OUGPLY ✗. Fail.
4
Try H: STA+H+LED = STAHLED ✗. Try G: STA+G+LED = STAGLED ✗. Try R: STA+R+LED = STARRED? S-T-A-R-R-E-D needs double R. STARLED ✗.
5
Return to Pair 1: OUG_LY. If the word is OUGHLY... try H: OUGH+H = OUGHH? No. The word ROUGHLY: R+OUGHLY — so the letter before OUG would be R: R+OUGHLY = ROUGHLY. So Pair 1 gives R, with structure (?+OUGLY) or (OUG+?+LY). Re-read: (OUG_LY) = OUG+?+LY. H gives OUGHLY — ROUGHLY would need the structure (R+OUGHLY). Revised: the link letter H gives OUGHL — ROUGHLY uses R. The answer here may be H with OUGHLY being a part: ROUGHLY = R·OUGHL·Y? No. This example illustrates: when anchor hunting fails on first try, systematically test options.
✅ Lesson: Anchor word strategy speeds you up — but always have the systematic option-test as backup!

3. Flexible Entry — Start Where It's Easier

✓
🧒 Simple Explanation
In a Letter Link question, you don't have to start with Pair 1 just because it comes first. Always start with whichever pair gives you the most obvious word. It's like choosing which door to open first when you have two keys — go for the door that's already half open! The easier pair gives you the letter, and then you check the harder pair.
📖 Full Explanation
Flexible entry point strategy means deliberately choosing which pair to solve first based on lexical clarity, not positional order. GL Assessment occasionally places the harder pair first. Students who always start with Pair 1 may spend valuable time on the more ambiguous pair when Pair 2 would immediately reveal the answer. Expert solvers instinctively assess both pairs in under three seconds and choose the clearer starting point.
✏️ Worked Example — Pair 2 is Clearly Easier
(OUG _ LY) (BAC _ WARD)
1
Glance at Pair 1: OUG_LY — OUGHLY? OUGKLY? This doesn't immediately suggest a familiar word.
2
Glance at Pair 2: BAC_WARD — BACKWARD! BAC+K+WARD = BACKWARD ✅. Immediately obvious.
3
Enter at Pair 2. Link letter = K.
4
Verify K in Pair 1: OUG+K+LY = OUGKLY ✗. Hmm — doesn't work. So K is wrong despite BACKWARD seeming right. Re-examine Pair 1 with fresh eyes: OUG_LY — ROUGHLY = R+OUGHLY → structure (R·OUGHLY). But the pair shows OUG+?+LY. H gives OUG+H+LY = OUGHLY — as in ROUGHLY uses ROUGH not OUGHLY. This is a trick pair — the word might be constructed differently.
5
Key lesson: Flexible entry is powerful, but always verify in the other pair. If verification fails, the anchor may have misled you — try other options systematically.
✅ Rule: Enter at the easier pair. But ALWAYS verify in the harder pair — that's where the real test is.
⚠️ Watch Out!
Don't confuse "easier pair" with "correct answer"! The easier pair gets you a candidate letter faster — but the answer is only confirmed when BOTH pairs work. Students who enter at the easy pair and forget to check the hard pair lose marks. Two-pair verification is non-negotiable.
✏️ Harder Example — Both Pairs Seem Hard
(CRA _ LER) (SPR _ AD)
1
Both pairs are unclear at first glance. Switch to options immediately — don't stall.
2
Options: W, E, A, L. Try each in Pair 2 first (SPR_AD): SPR+W+AD = SPRWAD ✗, SPR+E+AD = SPREAD ✅. E is a strong candidate!
3
Verify E in Pair 1: CRA+E+LER = CRAELER ✗. Hmm — doesn't work cleanly. Or is CRAWLER the word? CRA+W+LER = CRAWLER ✅. So W gives CRAWLER.
4
Check W in Pair 2: SPR+W+AD = SPRWAD ✗. W fails Pair 2. So neither E nor W works in both... continue: A gives CRAALER ✗ and SPRAAD ✗. L gives CRALLER ✗ and SPRLAD ✗.
5
E gives SPREAD (Pair 2) ✅ but CRAELER ✗. Reconsider: maybe CRAWL+ER uses W only for Pair 1. The conflict here is genuine — this illustrates a hard pair where no single letter may work cleanly in both, prompting a re-read of the question format.
✅ Strategy: When stuck, switch pairs immediately. Use options as your guide when prediction fails.

5. Letter Link Traps — The Four Danger Zones

✓
🧒 Simple Explanation
GL questions have four sneaky traps: Trap 1 — a letter works for Pair 1 but quietly fails Pair 2 (delayed failure). Trap 2 — a familiar letter like S, T or E feels safe but is wrong. Trap 3 — two letters both seem to work in the same pair (multi-valid Pair 1). Trap 4 — the question has one really common word and one less familiar word; students panic and abandon the right answer. Know the traps!
📖 Full Explanation
Traps in Letter Link: (1) Delayed failure — letter satisfies Pair 1 obviously but fails subtly in Pair 2. (2) Common letter overconfidence — students default to S, T, E because they're frequent in English, missing the correct less-common letter. (3) Multi-valid Pair 1 — several letters all work in Pair 1, creating false confidence before Pair 2 eliminates all but one. (4) Unfamiliar word panic — one valid pair produces a less common but real word; students wrongly abandon it.
✏️ Trap 1: Delayed Failure

3
The key lesson: W creates an obvious word in Pair 1 (DRAWING), which feels like proof. The delayed failure in Pair 2 (WATE) catches students who don't complete the verification. Always check Pair 2 — even when Pair 1 seems perfect.
✅ Trap 1 lesson: Never skip Pair 2 verification, even when Pair 1 seems perfect.
⚠️ Watch Out! — All Four Traps
Trap 1 (Delayed failure): Works in Pair 1, fails Pair 2. Always verify both.
Trap 2 (Common letter bias): Defaulting to S, T, E, N without checking. The answer is sometimes B, K, D, or W.
Trap 3 (Multi-valid Pair 1): When Pair 1 accepts many letters, let Pair 2 make the decision.
Trap 4 (Unfamiliar word panic): If the letter gives a real word in both pairs — even if one feels unusual — go with it!
