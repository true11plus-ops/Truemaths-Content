1. The Core Method — How to Fill the Gap

✓
🧒 Simple Explanation
You see a word with a gap in it — like M _ _ _ K. Your job is to fill in the missing letters to make a real word. Think of it like a jigsaw puzzle: you can already see some pieces (the letters at the start and end), and you need to find the piece in the middle that fits perfectly. Use the sentence around it to give you a big hint!
📖 Full Explanation
In GL Assessment Complete the Word questions, you are given a word with letters missing from the middle, start, or end — shown as dashes or underscores. You choose from five options to complete it. The completed word must: (1) form a real, correctly spelled word, and (2) make sense in the context of the sentence. Both conditions must be satisfied — one alone is not enough.

⚠️ Watch Out!
Don't commit to your first guess! The sentence might make you think of a word, but always check it against ALL the options. If a different option also makes a real word, you must test both in the sentence to see which one fits the meaning. Making a guess and stubbornly sticking to it is how students lose marks.
✏️ Harder Example — When Two Options Look Tempting
The scientist studied the MO _ _ _ LE structure carefully.
1
Context: "scientist… structure carefully" → probably a word from science. Think: MOLECULE!
2
Fragment analysis: MO + ??? + LE. How does MOLECULE split? MO-LECU-LE → so the missing part is LECU? That's 4 letters. But the gap might be 3. Reconsider: MO + LEC + ULE? Or MOLE + C + ULE? The split might not be where you expect!
3
Test options systematically: (A) LEC → MO+LEC+LE = MOLECLE ✗. (B) DUL → MODUULE? ✗. (C) OLE → MOOLELE? ✗. (D) ECU → MOECULE? ✗. Hmm. Go back — maybe the fragment is MO___ULE not MO___LE.
4
Recheck the gap: MO___ULE. Now: MO + LEC + ULE = MOLECULE ✅. The gap was LEC, and the second fixed chunk was ULE not just LE.
5
Lesson: Sometimes the split between the known letters and the gap isn't where you assume. Be flexible — rethink the split if your first read fails.
✅ Answer: LEC → MO + LEC + ULE = MOLECULE

2. Context Clues — Let the Sentence Help You

✓
🧒 Simple Explanation
The sentence around the missing word is like a treasure map — it gives you clues! Look for topic words (like "doctor", "hospital", "medicine" — that's a medical sentence). Look for describing words (like "shiny", "huge", "ancient"). Look for action words (like "climbed", "built", "sang"). Each clue narrows down what the missing word could be.
📖 Full Explanation
Context-based solving uses the semantic information in the sentence to predict or validate the missing word. Key context indicators include: subject domain (science, sport, nature), descriptive adjectives, verbs and their typical objects, and positional words (above, below, inside). Strong context readers predict a category of word before looking at options — this makes the search faster and more accurate.
✏️ Worked Example — Reading Context
The doctor gave him a P _ _ _ ON to take twice a day.
1
Topic words: "doctor" → medical. "take twice a day" → this is something you take like medicine.
2
Predict category: Something a doctor gives you that you "take" → medicine, tablet, pill, potion, prescription.
3
Fragment P___ON: Which of my category words fits? PotION → P + OTI + ON? POTION fits! P + OTI + ON = POTION ✅.
4
Test: "The doctor gave him a POTION to take twice a day." ✅ Makes sense in a fantasy/medicine context.
✅ Answer: OTI → P + OTI + ON = POTION
⚠️ Watch Out!
"Predict lightly — don't marry your guess!" Context gives you a good starting point, but never lock yourself in. If you predict "PILL" but the fragment doesn't fit PILL, don't panic — just move on. Weak students waste time defending a wrong prediction. Predict → test → if it fails, let go and try options instead.
✏️ Harder Example — Context Narrows to One
The surfer rode the enormous _ A V _ _ to the shore.
1
Context: "surfer… rode… shore" → something a surfer rides → WAVE!
2
Fragment _ A V _ _ : W + A + V + E + S? Let me count: that's 5 letters total with A and V given. _AV__ → WAVES fits: W + AV + ES → WAVES.
3
Options check: (A) WES (B) WEN (C) WEL (D) WEP → WAVES (WES?). Actually _AV__ → W + AV + ES = WAVES → missing = WES. WAVEN, WAVEL? No. WAVES ✅.
4
Context trap check: "enormous" → could also be CAVERN? C + A + V + E + R + N? No — that's 6 letters and doesn't fit surfer context. Context rules it out.
✅ Answer: WES → W + AV + ES = WAVES. Context removed all doubt!

3. Option-First Strategy — Work Backwards

✓
🧒 Simple Explanation
Instead of guessing the word first, flip it around — look at the answer options and build the full word from each one, then test which full word makes sense. It's like having five keys and trying each one in the lock. You slot each option into the gap, check if it spells a real word, then check if that word fits the sentence.
📖 Full Explanation
The option-driven construction strategy starts from the answer choices rather than predicting the word. For each option, you build the complete word by combining the known letters + the option letters, then check: (1) Is this a real, correctly spelled word? (2) Does this word fit the sentence meaning? This method is fastest when context prediction is unclear or when multiple predictions seem possible.
✏️ Worked Example — Option-First in Action
She climbed the LA _ _ _ R to reach the roof.
🔄 Option-First: Build & Test Each Option
(A) DDE
LADDER → LADDER ✅ real word
(B) FFE
LAFFER → LAFFER ✗ not a common word
(C) NDE
LANDER → LANDER ✗ (possible but doesn't fit context)
(D) GGE
LAGGER → LAGGER ✗ not standard
5
Context test: LADDER — "climbed the LADDER to reach the roof" ✅ Perfect. A ladder is exactly what you climb to reach a roof.
✅ Answer: DDE → LA + DDE + R = LADDER
⏱️ Speed Tip — 10-Second Rule
If you haven't predicted the word within 10 seconds of reading the sentence, switch to Option-First immediately. Don't waste time staring at the blank. Try each option, build the word, check for real word + good meaning. This is usually faster than forcing a prediction.
⚠️ Watch Out!
Instant rejection: if a combination creates an impossible letter cluster, reject it immediately. For example, if building the word gives you "PXYR" or "BNTLE" in the middle — that's unpronounceable, so not a word. You don't need to test it further. Quick rejection saves precious seconds.
✏️ Harder Example — Rejecting Impossible Clusters
The artist used a fine P _ _ _ EL to paint.
🔄 Quick Rejection in Action
(A) XYR
PXYREL → PXYREL ✗ impossible cluster — REJECT instantly
(B) AIS
PAISEL → PAISEL ✗ not a word
(C) AST
PASTEL → PASTEL ✅ real word — test in sentence
(D) IXZ
PIXZEL → PIXZEL ✗ impossible cluster — REJECT instantly
5
Context: "artist… fine… to paint" → PASTEL is a soft chalk-like art medium. ✅ "The artist used a fine PASTEL to paint" — perfect!
✅ Answer: AST → P + AST + EL = PASTEL

4. Dual Validation — Real Word AND Right Meaning

✓
🧒 Simple Explanation
You need to pass TWO tests, not one. Test 1: Is it a real word? (Could you find it in a dictionary?) Test 2: Does it fit the sentence? (Does the meaning make sense?) Sometimes you'll find a real word that doesn't fit the meaning — that's a trap! You must pass both tests to get the mark.
📖 Full Explanation
Dual validation requires confirming both lexical validity (the word exists and is correctly spelled) and semantic appropriateness (the word's meaning fits the sentence context). GL Assessment trap options frequently include real words that pass the lexical check but fail the semantic check. Students who only test "is this a word?" without checking meaning fall into this trap regularly.
✏️ Worked Example — Two Real Words, One Correct
The gardener used a S _ _ DE to move the soil.
1
Options: (A) PA → SPADE, (B) HA → SHADE, (C) BL → SBLDE ✗ (reject immediately), (D) LI → SLIDE.
2
Real word check: SPADE ✅ SHADE ✅ SLIDE ✅. Three real words — which one fits?
3
Meaning check: "gardener… move the soil" → a SPADE is a digging tool ✅. A SHADE gives shadow ✗ (doesn't move soil). A SLIDE is playground equipment ✗.
4
Only SPADE passes both tests. SHADE and SLIDE pass the word test but fail the meaning test.
✅ Answer: PA → S + PA + DE = SPADE. Both real word AND correct meaning ✅
⚠️ Watch Out!
The "real word, wrong meaning" trap is the most common way students lose marks. When you find an option that makes a real word, don't stop — ask "does this word make sense HERE, in this sentence?" If it doesn't fit the meaning perfectly, it's the trap. Keep testing the other options.
✏️ Harder Example — Overfitting Context
The team celebrated their VI _ _ _ RY with a parade.
1
Context prediction: "team celebrated" → winning → VICTORY seems obvious.
2
But check options: (A) CTO → VICTORY ✅ (B) SIO → VISION ✗ wrong end (C) GIL → VIGLRY ✗ (D) NTA → VINTAGE? VI+NTA+RY = VINTARY? ✗.
3
Dual validation: VICTORY = real word ✅ AND fits "team celebrated with a parade" ✅. No other option passes both tests.
4
Lesson: Even when context prediction is easy, still dual-validate. "Predict lightly" — use context to guide you, but always confirm with both tests.
✅ Answer: CTO → VI + CTO + RY = VICTORY. Real word + correct meaning ✅

5. GL Traps — The Four Danger Zones

✓
🧒 Simple Explanation
GL questions have sneaky traps built in to catch students who rush. There are four main danger zones: Trap 1 — a real word with the wrong meaning. Trap 2 — a word that sounds right but is spelled wrong. Trap 3 — a false common ending (like -ING or -TION) that leads you astray. Trap 4 — choosing the familiar word instead of the correct one. Know the traps, avoid the traps!
📖 Full Explanation
The four GL trap categories: (1) Semantic mismatch — real word, wrong context. (2) Phonetic deception — sounds correct but the spelling option doesn't match how it sounds. (3) False prefix/suffix — a standard word part (RE-, -TION, -ING) tempts students into building an incorrect word. (4) Common word bias — students favour familiar words over the correct but slightly less common answer.
✏️ Trap 1: Semantic Mismatch
The chef ST _ _ RED the sauce carefully.
1
Options: A → STARRED, I → STIRRED, O → STORRED, U → STURRED
2
STARRED = real word ✅ (to be in a film). "Chef STARRED the sauce"? ✗ Wrong meaning — chefs don't star sauce.
3
STIRRED = real word ✅ (to mix with a spoon). "Chef STIRRED the sauce carefully"? ✅ Perfect meaning.
4
STARRED is the trap — it's a real word but the meaning is wrong. STIRRED passes both tests.
✅ Answer: I → ST + I + RRED = STIRRED. Real word ✅ Correct meaning ✅

6. Hard Splits — When the Gap Isn't Where You Think

✓
🧒 Simple Explanation
Sometimes the missing bit is in a tricky place — like the middle of a long word, or it includes letters you didn't expect. The fixed letters (the ones you can see) might look like they belong to one part of the word, but actually they belong to a different part. When your first idea doesn't work — be flexible! Try reading the gap differently.
📖 Full Explanation
Non-obvious internal splits occur when the gap boundary doesn't align with morpheme boundaries (prefixes, roots, suffixes). Students who assume the gap is always between natural word parts will misparse the fragment. The solution is to: (1) try the obvious split first, (2) if that fails, rethink where the known letters end and the gap begins, and (3) test alternative parses of the same fragment. Flexibility over assumption.
✏️ Worked Example — The MOLECULE Problem Revisited
The scientist studied the MO _ _ _ LE structure.
1
First read: Frame = MO + ??? + LE. Options include LEC. Try: MO + LEC + LE = MOLECLE ✗ Not a word.
2
Rethink the split: What if the ending isn't just LE but ULE? MO + ??? + ULE. Now: MO + LEC + ULE = MOLECULE ✅!
3
The key insight: The given letters were MO___LE where the gap swallowed the CU from MOLECULE. The actual split in the word is MO-LEC-ULE but the shown frame was MO___ULE (with ULE as the fixed end, not LE).
4
Rule: When option-building fails, consider that the fixed letters might extend into what you thought was the gap. Reparse and try again.
✅ Answer: LEC → MO + LEC + ULE = MOLECULE. The split wasn't where it first appeared!
⚠️ Watch Out!
Flexible thinking, not stubbornness! If your first interpretation of the gap fails with every option, don't panic — just try a different read of where the known letters end. Count the total word length the option would create, and see if that matches a real word you know from context. Length awareness saves you here.

1
Context: "light… split into colours" → this is physics/science → a PRISM!
2
Fragment: PR + ??? + M. Options: IS → PR + IS + M = PRISM ✅. Try others: PR + AS + M = PRASM ✗. PR + OS + M = PROSM ✗.
3
PRISM might be less familiar than MILK or MEDAL — but context + structure both confirm it. Don't reject unfamiliar words if the evidence is strong!
4
Vocabulary stretch: GL uses words like PRISM, CIPHER, LUMEN, SUMMIT. If you know the topic, you can get these even if the words feel slightly unusual.
✅ Answer: IS → PR + IS + M = PRISM. Less common, but context confirms it!
