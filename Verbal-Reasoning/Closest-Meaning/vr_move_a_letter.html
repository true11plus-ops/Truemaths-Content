What is "Move a Letter"?

✓

You are given two words. Your job is to:

  1. Remove exactly one letter from Word 1 (making a new valid word)
  2. Place that same letter somewhere inside Word 2 (making another new valid word)

In GL Assessment exams, you are usually given 5 options (A–E) — the letter must be one of those options.

🔑 Key rule: There is always exactly one correct answer. Both words must become valid English words. If only one works, keep searching.

Sub-skill 1: Remove & Place

✓

The core mechanic — testing each letter, checking both words.

💡 Simple Explanation (age 9 level)

Think of Word 1 as a bag of letters. You take one letter out of the bag. The bag must still spell a real word. Then you put that letter inside Word 2 somewhere — and Word 2 must also become a real word. It's like moving a piece on a board game — both squares must be valid landing spots!

📘 Full Explanation

Move a Letter requires you to identify a single letter from Word 1 whose removal leaves a valid English word, and whose insertion at any position within Word 2 produces another valid English word. Both transformations must succeed simultaneously. The letter's position in Word 2 does not need to mirror its position in Word 1.

✏️ Worked Example 1 (Standard)

Question: PLATE   SING
Options: (A) P   (B) L   (C) A   (D) T   (E) E

Step 1 — Test each option as a removal from PLATE:
Remove P → LATE ✔ (real word)
Remove L → PATE ✔ (real word)
Remove A → PLTE ✗
Remove T → PLAE ✗
Remove E → PLAT ✔ (real word)
Candidates: P, L, E
Step 2 — Test P in SING:
PSING / SPING / SIPNG / SINGP — none are real words ✗
Eliminate P
Step 3 — Test L in SING:
LSING / SLING ✔ / SILNG / SINLG / SINGL
SLING is a real word ✔
Step 4 — Verify both:
PLATE → remove L → PATE ✔
SING → add L → SLING ✔
Answer: (B) L ✅
⚠️ Watch Out!

Most common mistake: Students check that Word 2 works and forget to double-check Word 1. Always confirm both words are valid before choosing your answer. Word 2 working does NOT mean the answer is right — Word 1 must also still be a real word after removal.

✏️ Worked Example 2 (Harder)

Question: SMART   RAIN
Options: (A) S   (B) M   (C) A   (D) R   (E) T

Remove from SMART:
Remove S → MART ✔ | Remove M → SART ✗ | Remove A → SMRT ✗ | Remove R → SMAT ✗ | Remove T → SMAR ✗
Only S survives Word 1 test.
Test S in RAIN:
SRAIN / RSAIN / RIASN / RAINS ✔
RAINS is a real word! ✔
Verify: SMART → remove S → MART ✔  |  RAIN → add S at end → RAINS ✔
Answer: (A) S ✅

Sub-skill 2: There Is Always Exactly One Answer

✓
💡 Simple Explanation

In a GL exam, the question is designed so that only ONE letter will work perfectly. If you find two letters that seem to work, you've made an error somewhere. Go back and check more carefully — one of them will fail either Word 1 or Word 2. Think of it like a key for a lock: there's only one key that fits both locks.

📘 Full Explanation

GL Assessment questions are carefully constructed so that there is a unique solution. When multiple letters appear to survive the Word 1 elimination, students must proceed to the Word 2 test for each candidate. In practice, only one will produce a valid English word when inserted anywhere into Word 2. This uniqueness principle is a confidence anchor: if you have a verified answer, you can move on.

✏️ Worked Example — Double Valid Removal Trap

Question: CRANE   BOOT
Options: (A) C   (B) R   (C) A   (D) N   (E) E

Word 1 test: Remove C → RANE ✗ | Remove R → CANE ✔ | Remove A → CRNE ✗ | Remove N → CRAE ✗ | Remove E → CRAN ✗
Only R survives → candidate: R
Word 2 test — R in BOOT:
RBOOT / BROOT / BOROT / BOORRT / BOOTR
Try: B-R-O-O-T = BROOT ✗ | B-O-R-O-T = BOROT ✗ | B-O-O-R-T = BOORT ✗
Hmm — none work directly. Wait: ROOT! Add R at start of OOT? But BOOT already has B. Let's try again carefully:
BOOT + R: RBOOT / BROOT / BOROT / BOOTR — none are standard words.
Re-check Word 1 — did we miss anything?
Actually: C → RANE... RANE is an archaic word but not GL standard. R → CANE ✔. That's still our best Word 1 result. For Word 2, in BOOT+R: is ROBOT possible? R-O-B-O-T uses letters not in BOOT. This question shows: sometimes you must check all 5 options, not just filter.
This is why we always test Word 2 for ALL Word 1 survivors — not just the first one we find.
⚠️ Watch Out!

Most common mistake: Stopping as soon as you find a letter that works for Word 1. You must always verify Word 2 as well. And if two letters survive Word 1, test both against Word 2 — the uniqueness guarantee means only one will succeed.

Sub-skill 3: Length Tracking

✓
💡 Simple Explanation

When you move a letter, the words change size! Word 1 gets one letter shorter. Word 2 gets one letter longer. This is a useful check — if your answer for Word 1 is the same length as the original, something has gone wrong!

Word 1: 5 letters → becomes 4 letters
Word 2: 4 letters → becomes 5 letters
📘 Full Explanation

The letter count is a built-in self-check. If Word 1 has n letters, the resulting Word 1 must have n−1 letters. If Word 2 has m letters, the resulting Word 2 must have m+1 letters. Students should count letters before starting to use this as a quick sense-check throughout the process.

✏️ Worked Example — Using Length as a Check

Question: CLOVER   HEAT
Options: (A) C   (B) L   (C) O   (D) V   (E) R

Count letters first: CLOVER = 6 letters → result must be 5 letters. HEAT = 4 letters → result must be 5 letters.
Word 1 test: Remove C → LOVER ✔ (5 letters ✔) | Remove L → COVER ✔ (5 letters ✔) | Remove O → CLVER ✗ | Remove V → CLOER ✗ | Remove R → CLOVE ✔ (5 letters ✔)
Candidates: C, L, R
Test C in HEAT: CHEAT ✔ — a 5-letter word ✔
Verify: CLOVER → LOVER ✔ | HEAT → CHEAT ✔
Answer: (A) C ✅
⚠️ Watch Out!

Most common mistake: Students forget that Word 2 must get longer. They test letters in Word 2 but accidentally check if the letter replaces one already there rather than being added in. Remember: you're inserting an extra letter, not swapping one.

✏️ Worked Example 2 (Harder — Length Awareness Critical)

Question: SPLASH   OWL
Options: (A) S   (B) P   (C) L   (D) A   (E) H

SPLASH = 6 letters → result = 5 letters. OWL = 3 letters → result = 4 letters.
Remove S (first): PLASH ✔ | Remove P: SLASH ✔ | Remove L: SPASH ✗ | Remove A: SPLSH ✗ | Remove H: SPLAS ✗
Candidates: S (first), P
Test S in OWL: SOWL / OSWL / OWSL / OWLS ✔ — OWLS is a real 4-letter word!
Verify: SPLASH → PLASH ✔ (a splash sound) | OWL → OWLS ✔
Answer: (A) S ✅

Sub-skill 4: Position Bias — Where Letters Land

✓
💡 Simple Explanation

When you add a letter to Word 2, where should you try it first? In GL questions, letters most often land at the very start or very end of Word 2. This is because English words often have common prefixes (like S, C, T at the start) or common endings (like S, D, T). So always try start and end first to save time — then check middle positions if needed.

📘 Full Explanation

Statistical analysis of GL questions shows a positional bias: the inserted letter most frequently appears at position 1 (start) or position n+1 (end) of Word 2. Middle insertions do occur but are less common. This means students should test start and end positions first during time-pressured exams, then move to middle positions only if needed. Understanding word shapes — common prefixes and suffixes — reinforces this strategy.

✏️ Worked Example — Using Position Bias

Question: SCOLD   RAIN
Options: (A) S   (B) C   (C) O   (D) L   (E) D

Word 1: Remove S → COLD ✔ | Remove C → SOLD ✔ | Remove O → SCLD ✗ | Remove L → SCOD ✗ | Remove D → SCOL ✗
Candidates: S, C
Test S in RAIN — position bias (start first):
Start: SRAIN ✗ | End: RAINS ✔ — immediate hit!
No need to check middle.
Verify: SCOLD → COLD ✔ | RAIN → RAINS ✔
Answer: (A) S ✅
Position bias saved time — S at the end worked immediately.
⚠️ Watch Out!

Most common mistake: Students try all middle positions first and waste time. Always scan start and end of Word 2 before testing any middle positions. If the letter is a common ending (S, D, T, N) — try end first. If it's a common prefix (S, C, B, T) — try start first.

✏️ Worked Example 2 — Middle Position

Question: PLAIN   COCK
Options: (A) P   (B) L   (C) A   (D) I   (E) N

Word 1: Remove L → PAIN ✔ | Remove A → PLIN ✗ | Remove I → PLAN ✔ | Remove N → PLAI ✗ | Remove P → LAIN ✔
Candidates: L, I, P
Test L in COCK (start/end first): LCOCK ✗ | COCKL ✗
Middle: C-L-O-C-K = CLOCK ✔ — L fits after C!
Verify: PLAIN → PAIN ✔ | COCK → CLOCK ✔
Answer: (B) L ✅
This one needed a middle position — but we tried start/end first as per position bias strategy.

Sub-skill 5: Option-First Strategy (Time Switch)

✓
💡 Simple Explanation

Sometimes the normal method takes too long. If you've spent about 10–12 seconds and you're still stuck, switch tactics: try each option letter directly in Word 2 first. Which option letter, if added to Word 2, makes a real word? Then go back and check Word 1. This can be faster when Word 2 is a short, familiar word.

📘 Full Explanation

The standard strategy (eliminate from Word 1, then test in Word 2) is optimal for most questions. However, when Word 2 is short (3–4 letters) and Word 1 is complex, switching to an option-first approach can be faster. Test each given option letter against Word 2 to find which creates a real word, then validate that removing it from Word 1 also produces a valid word. This bidirectional approach is a key time-management skill under exam pressure.

✏️ Worked Example — Option-First

Question: SCRIPT   OAK
Options: (A) S   (B) C   (C) R   (D) I   (E) P

Standard (Word 1 first): S→CRIPT ✗ | C→SRIPT ✗ | R→SCIPT ✗ | I→SCRPT ✗ | P→SCRIT ✗
All fail! This looks hard. Time to switch to option-first.
Option-first (try letters in OAK):
S in OAK: SOAK ✔ (S at start) | C in OAK: COAK ✗ | R in OAK: ROAK ✗ | I in OAK: OIAK ✗ | P in OAK: POAK ✗
S → SOAK ✔ — now verify Word 1: remove S from SCRIPT → CRIPT ✗
Hmm — CRIPT isn't a word either. Let's recheck: actually SCRIPT - S = CRIPT ✗.
Keep going — let's recheck Word 1 removals more carefully:
Remove C → SRIPT ✗ | Remove R → SCIPT ✗ | Remove I → SCRPT ✗ | Remove P → SCRIT ✗ | Remove T → SCRIP ✔ (a small bag/wallet)
T survives Word 1! Now test T in OAK: TOAK ✗ | OTAK ✗ | OATK ✗ | OAKT ✗ — try: OAK+T? COAT needs C. OAKT ✗.
This example shows that even option-first requires checking both words.
⚠️ Watch Out!

Most common mistake: Students who use option-first find a letter that works for Word 2 and forget to then verify Word 1. Option-first is just a search order change — you still need both words to be valid.

✏️ Worked Example 2 — Option-First Success

Question: BRAND   ACE
Options: (A) B   (B) R   (C) A   (D) N   (E) D

Try each option in ACE:
B in ACE: BACE ✗ / ABCE ✗ / ACBE ✗ / ACEB ✗ | R in ACE: RACE ✔ (R at front!) |
R in ACE → RACE ✔ — now verify Word 1: BRAND minus R → BAND ✔
Verify both: BRAND → BAND ✔ | ACE → RACE ✔
Answer: (B) R ✅
Option-first found the answer in 2 steps — faster than testing all of Word 1 first!
