What Is This Question Type?

Simple Explanation

Imagine two words, each with letters numbered 1, 2, 3, 4... Someone picks specific letters from each word using those numbers, and the letters combine to make a brand new word. Your job is to figure out the secret number rule — then use the same rule on a new pair of words to get the answer.


Think of it like a recipe: "take the 1st and 2nd ingredient from bowl A, and the 5th and 6th from bowl B." Once you know the recipe, you can make the dish again with any ingredients.

Full Explanation

In Letter Position Coding, a positional rule (a sequence of numbers) maps letters from two source words to create a target word. The combined numbering treats Word A as positions 1–4 and Word B as positions 5–8 (for 4-letter words). You must:

  1. Identify the positional pattern from the example pair
  2. Verify it produces the target word exactly
  3. Apply the identical rule to the new pair

Positional Extraction — The Core Method

Simple Explanation

Number each letter, find which numbered letters make the answer word, write the number pattern — then do the same with the new words. That's it. Simple counting!

Full Explanation

Positional extraction is the systematic process of mapping letter positions from a source pair to a target word, then applying the identified positional rule to a new pair. The rule is expressed as an ordered sequence of positions (e.g. 1-6-7-8) and must be applied in exactly that order.

Directional Flexibility — Word B Can Come First

Simple Explanation

Sometimes the rule picks letters from Word B before Word A. It's like being told to put on your shoes before your socks — unusual, but if that's the rule, that's the rule! The numbers tell you everything.

Full Explanation

The positional rule is not constrained to Word A → Word B order. A rule such as 6-7-1-2 extracts two letters from Word B first, then two from Word A. The numbering always remains fixed (Word A = 1–4, Word B = 5–8), but the extraction order can be in any sequence. Students must apply the rule in precisely the stated order.

✅ Worked Example — Reverse Order

Question: (LAMP, CORD → COLA) — what is (FISH, MENU → ?)

1
Number: LAMP=1234, CORD=5678
COLA: C=5, O=6, L=2, A=3 → Rule = 5-6-2-3
2
Note: the rule takes positions 5 and 6 (from Word B) first, then 2 and 3 (from Word A).
3
Apply to FISH(1234), MENU(5678):
M(5), E(6), I(2), S(3) = MEIS
✅ Answer: MEIS
Watch Out!

Most common mistake: Always assuming Word A letters come before Word B letters in the answer. If the answer doesn't work that way, check whether the rule runs in reverse order. The numbers never lie — follow them exactly.

Speed Optimisation — Exam-Smart Solving

Simple Explanation

You have about 30 seconds per question. You don't need to write out every single number. A quick scan of the answer word, spotting which words the letters came from, can crack the rule much faster.

Full Explanation

Rather than numbering all 8 positions every time, efficient candidates: (1) scan the target word letter by letter and identify which source word each came from; (2) note the positions of only the letters that appear in the target; (3) confirm the rule with a quick mental check. This reduces unnecessary writing under timed conditions.

✅ Speed Method Worked Example

Question: (PLAN, CORD → POND) — (SLIM, FARE → ?)

1
Fast scan POND: P from PLAN ✓ (pos 1), O from CORD ✓ (pos 6), N from PLAN ✓ (pos 4), D from CORD ✓ (pos 8). Rule = 1-6-4-8.
2
Apply fast: SLIM pos 1=S, FARE pos 6=A, SLIM pos 4=M, FARE pos 8=E → SAME.
✅ Answer: SAME
Watch Out!

Most common mistake: Going too fast and mis-identifying which word a letter came from when the same letter appears in both words. If a letter like "A" appears in both, slow down just for that letter and verify the position precisely.

Traps & Pitfalls — Know the Enemy

Simple Explanation

The exam writers know exactly how students make mistakes — and they design questions to trigger those mistakes. If you know the traps in advance, you can dodge them. Think of it like knowing where the puddles are before you run.

Full Explanation

GL Assessment question designers deliberately include distractors that exploit common errors: duplicate letters at different positions, answers that are real words but wrong, position-shift errors, and premature commitment to a plausible rule. Awareness of these traps — and deliberate verification habits — are what separate Mastery from Secure performance.

🪤 The Six Main Traps
1. Nice-looking wrong word — wrong rule gives a real word; correct rule gives something odd-looking. Always verify rule, not word appearance.
2. Duplicate letter confusion — A appears in position 2 AND position 7. The rule uses position 7, but you grabbed position 2. Check position, not just the letter.
3. Position shift error — you use 1-2-3-4 instead of 1-3-5-7 because you miscounted. Always verify against the example.
4. Early commitment — you lock on the first rule that looks right, refuse to reconsider. Keep the rule provisional until verified.
5. Partial word illusion — extracted letters spell a word after 3 letters. You stop early and miss the 4th. Extract ALL positions before checking.
6. Duplicate miscount — "E" appears twice; you pick the wrong one. Slow down on any repeated letter.
Watch Out!

The hardest trap: The "nice word" trap works because humans instinctively prefer real words. Train yourself to apply the rule and accept whatever it produces — then check the options to confirm.
