What Is Rotation?

✓
Simple Explanation

Imagine a clock hand. It doesn't get bigger, smaller, or change shape — it just spins around the centre. That's rotation. In these questions, a shape spins by a set amount. Your job is to work out how far it has spun, and pick the picture that shows it in its new position.

The key rule: the shape itself never changes. Same size, same shape, same markings — just pointing in a different direction.

Full Explanation

Rotation is a transformation in which every point of a figure turns by the same angle around a fixed centre point. Key properties: the shape, size, and internal structure are all preserved. Only the orientation changes. Rotation is distinct from reflection (which flips the figure) and translation (which slides it without turning). GL rotation questions ask you to identify the result of rotating a given figure by a specified or implied angle, or to identify which option among several is a valid rotation.

⚡ The Master Strategy — Follow This Order Every Time
  1. 1
    Find an anchor point. Pick one obvious feature — a corner, an arrow tip, a single dot. Track where that specific point goes after rotation.
  2. 2
    Check the smallest detail immediately. After finding your anchor, look at the tiniest feature (a dot, a small line, shading). Students lose marks by getting the big shape right and missing one small thing.
  3. 3
    Eliminate reflections first. If a shape is asymmetric (like L, F, R), check whether the answer is a mirror image. If you can't get there by spinning, it's a reflection — wrong answer.
  4. 4
    Verify relative positions. Ask: "Where is the dot compared to the arrow?" That relationship must be preserved after rotation.
  5. 5
    Tilting your head = last resort only. It can help check angles, but wastes time. Use it only to confirm an answer you already suspect.

Rotation Angles

✓
Simple Explanation

Think of a compass. An arrow starts pointing North. If you rotate it a quarter-turn clockwise, it now points East. Another quarter-turn: South. Another: West. Another: North again — back to the start.

Clockwise = the same direction clock hands move (right, down, left, up). Anticlockwise = the opposite. Each "quarter turn" = 90°. A half-turn = 180°.

Full Explanation

Angles in GL rotation questions are almost always multiples of 45°. The most common are 90°, 180°, and 270° (= 90° anticlockwise). Occasionally 45° appears. A key check: if a line was vertical before rotation by 90°, it will now be horizontal. If it was diagonal (45°), after 90° it will be diagonal in the other direction. Never guess an angle — verify by tracking what was vertical.

Angle verification trick: Find a line that was exactly vertical (pointing straight up) in the original. After 90° clockwise, that same line should now be exactly horizontal (pointing right). If it's diagonal instead — the rotation is 45°, not 90°.
⚠️ Watch Out!

Over-rotation error: Students often assume 180° when the actual rotation is 90°. The test: after 90° clockwise, what was on the LEFT of the shape should now be on the TOP. After 180°, what was on the left is now on the RIGHT. Always check which specific direction has moved where.

Mastery

The Anchor Method

✓
Simple Explanation

Imagine you put a sticker on the tip of a clock hand. If the hand spins, you can track exactly where your sticker ends up. That sticker is your "anchor point" — one specific part of the shape that you follow through the rotation.

Pick the most obvious, unique part of the shape: the tip of an arrow, the corner of an L-shape, or where a dot is. Watch where it goes. Then check the rest of the shape falls into place around it.

Full Explanation

The anchor method involves selecting one distinctive feature of the figure — typically a unique corner, endpoint, or marking — and tracking its movement during rotation. Once the anchor is correctly placed in the rotated position, the rest of the figure should follow naturally. The method works because all points rotate by the same angle around the same centre, so tracking one point constrains the entire figure's new orientation.

📐 Worked Example 1 — L-Shape, 90° Clockwise

An L-shape has its long side pointing UP and its foot pointing RIGHT. It is rotated 90° clockwise. What does it look like?

Original
(anchor = gold dot)
→
After 90° CW
(anchor moved right)
  1. 1
    Anchor = gold dot at the TOP of the long side. After 90° clockwise, top → right. So the anchor is now on the RIGHT side.
  2. 2
    The long side was vertical (up-down). After 90°CW, vertical → horizontal (left-right). Long side now runs left-right.
  3. 3
    The foot was pointing RIGHT. After 90°CW, right → down. Foot now points DOWN.
  4. 4
    Result: L with long side running left-right, foot pointing down, anchor dot on right end. ✓
⚠️ Watch Out!

Anchor at the wrong point: Students sometimes choose a non-unique feature as their anchor — like a corner shared by two arms of a shape. Choose the most extreme or distinctive point: a tip, a lone dot, a pointed corner. The more unique the anchor, the easier it is to track.

📐 Worked Example 2 — Harder: Arrow + Internal Dot, 180°

An arrow points RIGHT with a dot above it. After 180° rotation, what is the result?

  1. 1
    Anchor = arrowhead (tip), currently on the RIGHT. After 180°, right → left. Arrowhead now on the LEFT.
  2. 2
    The arrow's tail was on the LEFT. After 180°, left → right. Tail now on the RIGHT. Arrow now points LEFT.
  3. 3
    The dot was ABOVE the arrow. After 180°, above → below. Dot is now BELOW the arrow.
  4. 4
    Result: arrow pointing LEFT with a dot BELOW it.
Key lesson: "Above" becomes "below" after 180°. "Left" becomes "right". Every direction inverts. This is why 180° is described as "upside down and back-to-front" simultaneously.

Practice Questions

Starter

Developing

Secure
An arrow points RIGHT. A dot sits ABOVE the arrow. The whole figure is rotated 90° anticlockwise. After rotation: (i) where does the arrow point? (ii) where is the dot relative to the arrow?
Mastery
An arrow points RIGHT. A small circle is ABOVE the arrow. A triangle is BELOW the arrow. The whole figure is rotated 180°. What is the result?

Rotation vs Reflection

✓
Simple Explanation

Rotating a shape is like spinning a piece of paper on a table — it turns, but stays face-up. Reflecting a shape is like flipping the paper over — suddenly everything is backwards, like a mirror image.

The important rule: if a shape is not symmetric (e.g. the letter F or R), you can NEVER reach its mirror image by rotating. No matter how many times you spin it, a normal F will never become a backwards F. If you see a backwards F, that's a reflection — and wrong if the question asks for rotation.

Full Explanation

An asymmetric figure has a distinct "handedness" — like left and right gloves, which cannot be swapped by any rotation. A reflection reverses this handedness, producing the mirror image. For any asymmetric shape, the set of all rotations and the set of all reflections are completely separate — no rotation of a shape will ever produce its reflection. Symmetric shapes (squares, equilateral triangles, regular hexagons) are exceptions: some of their reflections look identical to rotations, so only the internal features (if any) distinguish them.

📐 Worked Example — Identifying Reflection Traps

You are shown an F-shape. Five options claim to be rotations. One is actually a reflection. How do you identify it?

Original F
90°CW ✓
Reflection ✗
  1. 1
    In the original F, the arms stick out to the RIGHT of the vertical stroke.
  2. 2
    After ANY rotation, the arms will still stick out in a consistent direction relative to the stroke's length — they never flip to the opposite side.
  3. 3
    If the arms appear on the OPPOSITE side of the stroke (like a backwards F), that's a reflection — it cannot be reached by rotation alone.
  4. 4
    Quick test: Trace the original on paper. Spin the paper — can you match the option? If yes = rotation. If no = reflection.
⚠️ Watch Out!

Orientation bias trap: Students sometimes think "upside down = different shape" and accept a reflection as a valid rotation. Remember: upside down (180°) IS a valid rotation. A 180° F still has its arms pointing in the consistent direction. Only when the arms switch sides has a reflection occurred. Test: after flipping a piece of paper face-down, does the tracing match the option?

Internal Details & Small Features

✓
Simple Explanation

Imagine you have a coin. Both sides look similar at first glance. But there's a tiny detail — a date, a mark — that tells you which side is which. In rotation questions, the "big shape" might look right in several options. The tiny detail inside is the clue that points to the one correct answer.

After you find your anchor, always immediately check the smallest detail: a shaded corner, a dot, a small line, which way a stripe runs. This is where most marks are won or lost.

Full Explanation

Internal details include: dots or circles within the shape, shading on specific sections (e.g. one quadrant filled), internal lines or stripes (including their orientation after rotation), small additional shapes, and the direction of asymmetric internal marks. Critically, stripe orientation rotates with the shape — if a shape has horizontal stripes and rotates 90°, the stripes become vertical. Detail neglect (ignoring small features) is the single most common cause of incorrect answers on rotation questions.

📐 Worked Example — Shading Rotation

A square has its TOP-RIGHT quadrant shaded. It rotates 90° clockwise. Which quadrant is now shaded?

  1. 1
    The shaded quadrant is in the TOP-RIGHT. Think of it as a dot in the top-right corner.
  2. 2
    After 90° clockwise, top→right and right→bottom. So the top-right corner moves to the bottom-right corner.
  3. 3
    The shaded quadrant is now the BOTTOM-RIGHT.
  4. 4
    Check: What was on the top moves right. What was on the right moves down. Top-right → right+down = bottom-right. ✓
⚠️ Watch Out!

Stripe direction trap: Students forget that stripes rotate with the shape. If a rectangle has vertical stripes and rotates 90°, the stripes are now horizontal. If it has diagonal stripes (like /) and rotates 90°, the stripes become (\). Always redraw the stripe direction after applying the rotation — never leave stripes unchanged.

📐 Worked Example 2 — Harder: Diagonal Stripes + 90°

A pentagon has "/" diagonal stripes (going bottom-left to top-right). It rotates 90° clockwise. What direction do the stripes run after rotation?

  1. 1
    Original stripes: "/" direction = 45° angle (bottom-left to top-right).
  2. 2
    After 90° clockwise, every direction rotates 90°CW. A "/" line's top-end was pointing top-right. After 90°CW, top-right → bottom-right.
  3. 3
    So the top of each stripe now points bottom-right, and the bottom points top-left. This gives a "\" stripe direction.
  4. 4
    Rule: "/" stripes + 90° rotation = "\" stripes. And "\" + 90° = "/".

Practice Questions

Starter

Secure

Mastery

Option B has "\" stripes and dot at bottom-left (reflection). Option C has "/" stripes and dot at top-right. Option D has "\" stripes and dot at top-left. If the rotation is 90° clockwise, which is the one correct option?

Multi-Element Rotation

✓
Simple Explanation

Imagine a toy with multiple pieces all glued together — an arrow, a dot, and a square all stuck in one unit. When you spin the whole toy, ALL the pieces spin together. They all rotate by the same amount, and their positions relative to each other stay the same.

The key question to ask: "Where is the dot compared to the arrow?" That relationship must stay the same after rotation — it should just be pointing in the new direction.

Full Explanation

Multi-element rotation involves figures with several distinct components (shapes, dots, arrows, lines) that all rotate as a rigid unit. The critical property to preserve is relative positioning — if element A is to the left of element B before rotation, then after rotation element A is still "to the left" of B in the new orientation (where "left" means in the direction perpendicular-left to the main shape's new pointing direction). Track at least two elements independently to confirm the entire figure has rotated correctly.

📐 Worked Example — Three-Element Figure, 90°CW

Figure: arrow points RIGHT. Circle is ABOVE the arrow. Square is BELOW the arrow. Rotate 90° clockwise.

  1. 1
    Arrow: points right → after 90°CW → points DOWN.
  2. 2
    Circle: was ABOVE the arrow. After 90°CW, "above" (in the original) becomes "to the right" (in the new orientation). Circle is now to the RIGHT of the arrow.
  3. 3
    Square: was BELOW the arrow. After 90°CW, "below" becomes "to the left". Square is now to the LEFT of the arrow.
  4. 4
    Result: Arrow points down, circle to the right, square to the left.
  5. 5
    Verify: "Where is circle compared to arrow?" Originally above. Now to the right. After 90°CW, above→right. ✓
⚠️ Watch Out!

Partial rotation trap: One common wrong answer rotates the main shape correctly but leaves one element (e.g. the circle) in its original position rather than rotating it too. Always check every element individually. If the arrow has rotated correctly but the dot is in the same place as before — that is a partial rotation trap and is wrong.

Practice Questions

Starter
An arrow points UP. A dot is to the RIGHT of the arrow. The whole figure rotates 90° clockwise. After rotation: (i) which way does the arrow point? (ii) where is the dot relative to the arrow?
Developing
Figure: a rectangle (taller than wide). A star is at the TOP of the rectangle. An X mark is at the BOTTOM. The whole figure rotates 90° anticlockwise. After rotation: where are the star and X mark?
Secure
A complex figure: arrow pointing RIGHT, circle ABOVE the arrowhead, triangle BELOW the tail of the arrow. Rotate 180°. What is the result?
Mastery

B=arrow down, circle to right, triangle to left. C=arrow up, circle to right, triangle to left. D=arrow down, circle to right, triangle to right (partial rotation trap). Which is the single correct option?

Symmetry Edge Cases

✓
Simple Explanation

A square looks the same after a 90° spin. A circle looks the same after ANY spin. So how do you find the rotation answer when the outer shape doesn't change? The trick is always hidden inside the shape — a dot, a stripe, a shaded corner. The outer shape is a decoy. The inside detail is the real clue.

Full Explanation

Symmetric shapes (square, circle, equilateral triangle, regular hexagon) have rotational symmetry — they look identical after certain rotations. For these shapes, the outer outline provides no information. The answer must be determined entirely from internal features. This is the symmetry trap: students see a square that "looks the same" and panic. The solution is to ignore the outer shape completely and treat the internal detail as the only shape that matters.

⚠️ Watch Out!

The "same shape" panic: When all options show the same outer shape (e.g. all squares), students sometimes give up or guess randomly. Don't. Just pretend the outer shape doesn't exist. Focus only on what's inside — where is the dot, which corner is shaded, which way does the internal arrow point. Treat the internal detail as a standalone rotation question.

Practice Questions

Starter
A circle has a dot in the TOP-LEFT of its interior. The circle is rotated 90° clockwise. The outer circle looks identical. Where is the dot now?
Developing
A square (all sides equal) has its TOP-RIGHT quadrant shaded dark. It is rotated 180°. The outer square looks identical. Which quadrant is now shaded?
Secure
An equilateral triangle (all sides equal, symmetric) has a small "X" mark near the middle of one side — the BOTTOM side. The triangle is rotated 120° clockwise (one-third turn, since equilateral triangles have 3-fold symmetry). Where is the X mark after rotation?
Mastery
A square has "/" diagonal stripes AND a dot in its top-left corner. The square is rotated 90° clockwise. The outer square looks identical. Which option correctly shows the result?

Rotation Sequences

✓
Simple Explanation

Sometimes a shape doesn't just rotate once — it rotates step by step, a little more each time. Like the minute hand of a clock: it moves steadily. In rotation sequence questions, you see a shape in several positions and need to find the pattern of rotation — how many degrees it spins each step — then predict the next position.

Full Explanation

Rotation sequences appear in matrix rows or in series questions where a shape is shown at multiple stages of rotation. You must identify the common rotation angle applied each step. Key skill: calculate the step size from two consecutive positions, then apply it to predict the next. Common step sizes: 45°, 90°, 135°, 180°. Watch for sequences that complete a full cycle (360°) and reset.

📐 Worked Example — Finding the Step Size

An arrow: Step 1 = pointing UP. Step 2 = pointing RIGHT. Step 3 = pointing DOWN. Step 4 = ?

  1. 1
    Step 1→2: up → right = 90° clockwise.
  2. 2
    Step 2→3: right → down = 90° clockwise. ✓ Confirmed: step size = 90°CW.
  3. 3
    Step 3→4: down → left = 90° clockwise. Step 4 = LEFT.
  4. 4
    Step 5 would be: left → up (back to start, full cycle complete).
⚠️ Watch Out!

Step size from one pair is not enough: Always verify your step size using at least two consecutive pairs before using it to predict. If Step 1→2 = 90°CW but Step 2→3 = 45°CW, the step size is NOT constant — there may be an alternating or accelerating pattern. Always check two pairs.

Practice Questions

Starter

Common Traps & Misconceptions

✓
⚠️ Trap 1: The Reflection Trap

One answer option is a mirror image of the correct rotation. For asymmetric shapes, the reflection looks convincingly similar but has reversed handedness. Always check: can you reach this by spinning, or only by flipping? If only by flipping — it's a reflection and wrong.

⚠️ Trap 2: The Partial Rotation Trap

The main shape rotates correctly, but one small element (a dot, a line) doesn't move — it stays in its original position. This option looks almost right. Always verify EVERY element has rotated, not just the main shape.

⚠️ Trap 3: The Over-Rotation Error

Students assume 180° when the rotation is 90°, or 90° when it's 45°. Always check a specific line: if a vertical line is now horizontal, that's 90°. If it's diagonal, that's 45°. Never estimate without checking a specific reference line.

⚠️ Trap 4: Detail Neglect

The big shape matches, so the student picks that option without checking the internal detail (a shaded corner, a dot, a stripe). The internal detail is almost always what separates the correct answer from the most tempting wrong answer. Check it last — but always check it.

⚠️ Trap 5: Symmetry Panic

All options show the same outer shape (e.g. all squares). Student panics and guesses. Solution: completely ignore the outer shape. Treat the internal feature as the only thing that exists and apply the rotation to that feature alone.
