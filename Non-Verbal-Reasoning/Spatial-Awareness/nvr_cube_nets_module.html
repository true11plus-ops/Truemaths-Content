🧒 Simple Explanation
Imagine you have a cardboard box. If you cut along its edges and flatten it out completely, you get a net — a flat shape made of 6 connected squares. The question is: if you fold it back up, which cube would you get? Think of it like origami — you're imagining the box folding itself shut in your mind.
📘 Full Explanation
A cube net is a two-dimensional arrangement of six squares (faces) connected edge-to-edge such that when folded along those edges, they form a closed cube. GL Assessment tests whether you can:
  • Identify which net produces a given labelled cube
  • Identify which cube results from a given net
  • Eliminate nets that are geometrically invalid
  • Track face positions and orientations through the folding process

Only 11 of the 35 possible hexomino arrangements are valid cube nets.

🗺️ The E·O·R Strategy
E — Eliminate Layouts: First, check if the net is even valid. Watch for the "Z-shape" or "S-shape" of 4 squares in a row with extra squares on the same side — these are invalid.
O — Find Opposites: Identify which faces are opposite each other. Opposite faces can NEVER be next to each other on the net. Lock these pairs in your mind before touching the answer options.
R — Check Rotation & Relationship: Confirm the orientation of face markings. A face pointing the right way in the wrong rotation is still wrong.
🎲 Sub-Skill 1: Reading a Net
🧒 Simple Explanation
Think of a cross-shaped net as a plus sign (+). The square in the very middle folds into the bottom of the box. The four squares touching it become the sides. The one square at the top of the column becomes the lid. Picture closing the box step by step — bottom first, then sides fold up, then lid drops on top.
📘 Full Explanation
A net is read by identifying a base face — typically the central square — and then tracking how each connected square folds relative to it. Squares connected by an edge will share that edge on the final cube. Squares connected diagonally (at a corner) will meet only at a vertex (point), not along an edge. This distinction is critical: diagonal contact in a net ≠ face-to-face contact on the cube.
📐 Worked Example 1 — Easy

A standard cross-shaped net has faces labelled 1–6. What face is opposite face 1 (the centre)?

Step 1: Face 3 is the base (centre column, middle row). Fold sides 2 and 4 up as left/right walls.
Step 2: Face 1 folds up as the top. Face 5 and 6 fold up as back and bottom.
Answer: Face 1 (top of column) ends up opposite face 5 (bottom of column). They are in the same straight line, separated by the base — so they are always opposite.
⚠️ Watch Out!
Diagonal ≠ adjacent. If two squares only touch at a single corner point (diagonal), they do NOT share an edge on the cube. Students often assume diagonal squares will become touching faces — they won't. Only edge-to-edge connections in the net create face-sharing on the cube.
📐 Worked Example 2 — Medium

An L-shaped net: squares in positions (row,col) — (1,1), (2,1), (3,1), (3,2), (3,3), (3,4). Is this a valid cube net?

Step 1 — Count squares: 6 squares ✓
Step 2 — Check connectivity: All squares are edge-connected ✓
Step 3 — Check for the "4-in-a-row with same-side branches" trap: The bottom row has 4 in a row. The column extends from position (3,1) upward — that's a branch on the LEFT end of the 4-in-row. This is actually the valid "L-tetromino + 2" shape ✓
Answer: Yes, this is a valid net. It folds correctly.

🔁 Sub-Skill 2: Identifying Opposite Faces
🧒 Simple Explanation
Hold a dice. The number on the top and the number on the bottom are opposite — you can never see both at the same time. On any cube, there are always exactly 3 pairs of opposite faces. If you know which faces you can see (up to 3), you already know which faces are opposite. The rule is simple: visible faces can NEVER be opposite each other.
📘 Full Explanation
In any cube, every face has exactly one opposite face — the face directly across the interior. These three pairs (top/bottom, front/back, left/right) share no edges or vertices. In a net, opposite faces are always separated by exactly one face between them in a straight line. The fastest way to identify opposite faces is:
  • Any two faces in the same straight column/row, with exactly one face between them, are opposite
  • Any face visible on a drawn cube cannot be opposite another visible face
  • Lock your three pairs BEFORE looking at answer options
📐 Worked Example 1

A cube shows: Top = ◆, Front = ●, Right = ▲. What is opposite ◆?

Step O1: List all visible faces: ◆, ●, ▲. These three cannot be opposite each other.
Step O2: The cube has 6 faces. The 3 hidden faces are opposite the 3 visible ones. ◆ is the top — its opposite is the BOTTOM (hidden). ● (front) is opposite the BACK (hidden). ▲ (right) is opposite the LEFT (hidden).
Answer: The face opposite ◆ is the bottom face (whatever symbol/label is placed there in the net).
Key Rule: "Visible faces can NEVER be opposite" — burn this into your memory.
⚠️ Watch Out!
Don't confuse "adjacent" with "opposite." On a cube, 4 faces are adjacent to any given face (they share an edge with it), and only 1 face is opposite. In a net, if two labelled squares share an edge, they will ALWAYS be adjacent on the cube — never opposite. Students often mistake close-by squares in the net as being opposite faces on the cube.
📐 Worked Example 2 — Harder

Net: Row of 3 (A–B–C), with D below B, E below D, and F to the right of C. Which face is opposite B?

Step 1: B is the middle of the top row. Identify the straight-line pairs: A and C are left/right of B — they'll fold to be opposite each other (left/right walls).
Step 2: D is directly below B — adjacent to B. E is below D — separated from B by D, so E is opposite B.
Step 3: F (right of C) will fold to become the top or bottom cap.
Answer: E is opposite B. They are in the same column with exactly one face (D) between them.

🔗 Sub-Skill 3: Adjacency & Chain Logic
🧒 Simple Explanation
Imagine three people standing in a line: Alice, Bob, and Charlie. Alice is next to Bob. Bob is next to Charlie. But is Alice next to Charlie? Not directly — there's Bob in between. On a cube, this is called chain adjacency: Face A touches Face B, Face B touches Face C — this tells you A and C are NOT opposite, because they're both sharing edges with B. Faces that are both adjacent to the same face cannot be opposite to each other.
📘 Full Explanation
Adjacency chains allow you to determine face relationships without folding. Rules:
  • If A is adjacent to B, and B is adjacent to C, then A and C cannot be opposite (they both border B, and a single face can only have one opposite)
  • If A is adjacent to B, then A and B cannot be opposite each other
  • Use the chain to narrow down which face must be opposite which
  • In a net, count the number of faces between two squares in the same line: 0 between them = adjacent; 1 between = opposite; 2 between = adjacent again (wrap-around)
📐 Worked Example 1

Net column (top to bottom): A – B – C – D. Branch: E to the right of B, F to the right of C. Which face is opposite A?

Step 1 — Column analysis: In a 4-square column (A–B–C–D), the two ends are always opposite. A (top) is opposite D (bottom).
Step 2 — Branches: E (beside B) and F (beside C) fold up as the remaining two faces. They will be opposite to each other.
Step 3 — Chain check: A is adjacent to B. B is adjacent to E. So A and E are both touching B → they cannot be opposite each other. ✓ Consistent with our conclusion (A is opposite D).
Answer: A is opposite D. E is opposite F. B is opposite C.
⚠️ Watch Out!
False Vertex Trap. When three squares meet at a single corner in the net, students think all three will be adjacent faces on the cube. But squares that only meet at a point (vertex) in the net do NOT share an edge on the cube — they only share a corner vertex. True adjacency requires a shared EDGE, not just a shared corner.
📐 Worked Example 2 — Chain Elimination

A cube's 3 visible faces are labelled P (top), Q (front), R (right). Identify all three opposite pairs.

Step 1: P, Q, R are all visible → they are pairwise adjacent (each pair shares an edge).
Step 2: Chain: P adjacent to Q, Q adjacent to R, R adjacent to P. None of these three can be opposite to any other of these three.
Step 3: The 3 hidden faces (call them P', Q', R') are opposite P, Q, R respectively.
Conclusion: P↔P' (bottom), Q↔Q' (back), R↔R' (left). The three hidden faces are also mutually adjacent (they share the hidden back-bottom-left vertex region).

🔄 Sub-Skill 4: Orientation & Rotation Tracking
🧒 Simple Explanation
Finding the right face isn't enough — it also has to be pointing the right way. Imagine an arrow on a piece of paper. If you fold that paper, the arrow might end up pointing left instead of up. This is orientation. When a question shows you a face with a marking on it, you need to check: is the marking in the right position AND pointing the right direction? Getting the face right but the rotation wrong = wrong answer.
📘 Full Explanation
When a face folds, its orientation (which way is "up" on that face) changes depending on the fold direction. Tracking orientation requires:
  • Fold direction awareness: Folding up, down, left, or right rotates the face by 90°
  • Anchor tracking: Fix one face as your reference point (usually the one you can see clearly on the shown cube), then track how each adjacent face folds relative to it
  • Orientation parity: A sequence of folds that is clockwise viewed from outside produces different orientations than anticlockwise — very advanced but appears in hardest GL questions
Common marks to track: arrows, diagonal lines, dots near edges, letters/numbers that can be rotated.
📐 Worked Example 1

A net has a square with an upward arrow (↑). It is directly to the RIGHT of the centre square. When folded, the centre square is the front face. What direction does the arrow point on the assembled cube?

Step 1 — Identify fold direction: The arrow square is to the RIGHT of centre (front). When folding, it folds around the right edge of the front face.
Step 2 — Apply fold rotation: The face folds 90° inward (to the right). The top of the arrow square becomes the right side in folded position. So ↑ becomes → (rotated 90° clockwise as viewed from outside).
Step 3 — Final orientation: The arrow on the right face of the cube points toward the FRONT (→ from the cube's right-face perspective means toward the viewer's left).
Answer: The arrow rotates 90° when the face folds to the right. Track the top of the arrow — it follows the fold direction.
⚠️ Watch Out!
Orientation blindness is the #1 source of errors in hard cube questions. Students find the correct face but pick the wrong option because they ignore the rotation. Always check: (1) Is this the right face? (2) Is the marking in the right position on the face? (3) Is the marking pointing the right direction? All three must match. If an answer has the right symbol but rotated 90° or 180°, it is wrong.
📐 Worked Example 2 — Hard

Net: Centre = plain square. Above centre = face with a dot in the TOP-LEFT corner. This face folds to become the TOP of the cube. When viewed from above, where is the dot?

Step 1: The face starts above the centre. It folds DOWN onto the top of the cube (folds inward, rotating 90° forward/toward viewer).
Step 2 — Track the dot: The dot was in the top-left of the net face. When the face folds forward, "top" in the net becomes the BACK edge of the top face. "Left" stays left.
Step 3: Dot lands at back-left when viewed from above.
Answer: The dot is in the back-left corner of the top face when the cube is assembled. Any answer showing the dot at front-left, front-right, or back-right is wrong.

↩️ Sub-Skill 5: Reverse Problems (Cube → Net)
🧒 Simple Explanation
Most questions show you a flat net and ask what cube it makes. But some questions flip it: they show you a cube and ask which net could have made it. Think of it like unfolding a cardboard box. You see a finished box — can you figure out which flat pattern it came from? Your job is to "unfold" the cube in your mind. Start with one face as your base, then imagine each other face peeling away and lying flat.
📘 Full Explanation
Reverse problems require different thinking from standard net-to-cube problems:
  • Work backwards: Start with the cube's visible faces. Choose one face as your "ground" and mentally unfold each adjacent face flat
  • Net equivalence: The same cube can be unfolded into multiple different valid nets — don't eliminate options just because the net looks different from what you expected
  • Opposite face rule still applies: In any valid net of the cube, opposite face pairs must be separated by exactly one face in a straight line
  • Rotation equivalence: A net and its 90°/180°/270° rotations are the same net — students wrongly eliminate rotated-but-correct nets
📐 Worked Example 1

A cube has: Top=★, Bottom=●, Front=▲, Back=■, Left=♦, Right=♣. Which net is correct?

Step 1 — Lock opposite pairs: ★↔●, ▲↔■, ♦↔♣
Step 2 — Check each net option: In a valid net, ★ and ● must be separated by exactly one face in a line. If any net places ★ and ● adjacent (sharing an edge), eliminate it immediately.
Step 3 — Check orientation: ★ must be in the correct orientation relative to ▲. If the net shows ★ rotated incorrectly relative to ▲'s position, eliminate it.
Step 4 — Accept rotation equivalence: If two nets appear different but have identical opposite pairs and identical adjacency structure, they are equivalent — accept the one with correct orientations.
⚠️ Watch Out!
Net rotation ≠ wrong net. Students frequently eliminate the correct answer because the net is rotated 90° from what they expected. A net and its rotated version are the same net. Before eliminating a net option, verify the opposite-face pairs match. If they do and only the overall rotation of the net differs, it is still correct.
📐 Worked Example 2 — Net Equivalence

Are these two nets the same: (1) Cross with top arm = ★, (2) Cross rotated 90° clockwise so the arm is now on the right = ★. Do they produce the same cube?

Answer: Yes. Rotating a net 90° produces the same cube in a rotated orientation. If the question is "which net could produce this cube" (from any orientation), both nets are valid. They are structurally identical — just rotated.
However: If the cube is shown in a specific orientation and you must match it exactly, then orientation of the net matters — you need the net that produces that exact orientation, not just any equivalent cube.
