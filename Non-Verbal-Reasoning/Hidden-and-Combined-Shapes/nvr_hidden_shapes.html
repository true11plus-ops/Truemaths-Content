Hidden Shapes

Find a small shape hidden inside a larger, more complex figure — even when it's disguised, rotated, or buried in clutter.

What is this question type? You are shown a small target shape on the left. Then you look at a larger, busier figure on the right — made of overlapping lines, shading, and extra detail. Your job is to find exactly where the small shape is hiding inside the big picture. The shape might be tilted, hidden in a corner, or disguised by surrounding lines.

🎯 The Fast GL Strategy (30 seconds max!)

1️⃣Spot ONE unique feature in the target shape — not the whole thing. Look for something unusual: a right angle, a very long side, a curved edge. This is your hunting clue.
2️⃣Scan to eliminate 3 zones fast. Look at each answer option and ask: "Can I see my hunting clue here?" Reject answers where it clearly isn't present. This "reject before confirm" approach is faster than confirming every option.
3️⃣Trace the full perimeter of your surviving option only. Count corners, check angles, verify side lengths. Does every line of the target shape exist in the larger figure?
4️⃣Check lines aren't borrowed. Every line of the hidden shape must use real lines from the figure — not imaginary ones you're drawing in your head.
1

Line Extension & Intersection

○
🌱 Simple Explanation

Imagine you're looking at a drawing of a spider's web. The web has lots of lines crossing each other. Your job is to find a triangle — but the triangle's sides are shared with the web's threads. The triangle doesn't have its own separate lines: it borrows the web's lines. You have to trace the threads carefully to find the shape hiding inside.

💡 Think of it like finding a word hidden in a word-search grid — the letters are already there, you just need to trace the right path.

📘 Full Explanation

Line extension occurs when lines in the larger figure extend beyond the boundary of the hidden shape, making it hard to see where the shape starts and ends. Intersection analysis means identifying which crossing-points (vertices) belong to the target shape. The hidden shape's edges are always real lines from the figure — they are never imaginary. You must identify the correct segment of each extended line that forms the target shape's boundary.

📐 Worked Example 1 — Foundation Level

Target shape: A right-angled triangle (one corner is a perfect square corner). Question: Is this triangle hidden in the figure below?

Target Shape
Figure (hidden shape highlighted in gold)
1

Find the unique feature: The target triangle has one right angle (square corner). Hunt for a square corner in the figure. I can see one at the bottom-left, where a horizontal and vertical line meet at 90°.

2

Trace the long side: From the bottom-left corner, the hypotenuse (slanted side) goes diagonally up-right. I can see a diagonal line in the figure going from bottom-left to top-right. ✓

3

Check line segments, not full lines: The figure has extra lines extending further. The hidden triangle only uses the segment of each line — not the full length. The dashed lines in the figure are extras that don't belong to the target shape.

4

Confirm: Yes — a right-angled triangle with exactly the same proportions IS hidden here, using real lines from the figure. The gold highlighted region shows it.

⚠️ Watch Out!
  • Don't use imaginary lines. Every side of the hidden shape must follow a real line in the figure. If you're "completing" a side that isn't there, you've made the Phantom Line mistake.
  • Extended lines are traps. When a line goes past the corner of the hidden shape, students often think the shape is "too big." Remember: you're only using a segment of that longer line.
📐 Worked Example 2 — Harder (GL Exam Level)

Target: An irregular quadrilateral (4 sides, no right angles). Trap: The figure has several crossing diagonals creating many false quadrilaterals.

Target Shape
Figure (correct hidden shape in gold)
1

Identify one unique feature: The target has a very short bottom-left side and a long right side. Hunt for that specific short-segment start point.

2

Reject three fast: Look at three of the four X-shaped crossing regions — none has the correct combination of a short-then-long side meeting at the right angles. Eliminated in under 10 seconds.

3

Confirm the survivor: Trace all four sides of the highlighted quadrilateral. Each side follows a real line segment. The shape has the same relative proportions as the target. Correct!

4

Why the trap works: The crossing diagonals create many four-sided regions. Students who try to confirm each one waste time. The fast-scan-then-reject approach cuts straight to the answer.

Negative Space Recognition

○
🌱 Simple Explanation

Imagine a biscuit cutter pressed into dough — the cut-out gap is a star shape. The star itself has been removed, but you can still see it in the gap left behind. In hidden shapes, sometimes your target shape is the empty space between filled-in regions, not a shape drawn with lines. You have to look at the gaps, not the solid parts.

💡 Like seeing a rabbit in the clouds — the shape isn't drawn; it's the space between other things.

📘 Full Explanation

Negative space is the unfilled region enclosed by the boundaries of other shapes or filled areas. In GL Assessment, figures sometimes use shading, hatching, or filled regions to disguise the target shape — which appears as a white or unshaded gap between the filled parts. You must recognise that the shape you're hunting may be defined by its surroundings, not by its own drawn outline.

📐 Worked Example 1 — Negative Space Foundation

Target: A small rectangle. Figure: Two filled (dark) L-shapes placed facing each other, leaving a white rectangular gap between them.

Target Shape
Figure (white gap = hidden rectangle)
1

Notice the shading. Most of the figure is dark/filled. Look at what's left white (unshaded). What shape do the white gaps make?

2

Trace the gap's boundary. The white gap in the centre is bordered on all four sides by straight edges of the dark regions. Four straight sides meeting at right angles → a rectangle!

3

Compare to target. The white gap is a rectangle with the same proportions as the target. ✓ The hidden shape is the negative space — the gap between the filled areas.

⚠️ Watch Out!
  • Don't only look at drawn lines. The hidden shape may have no lines of its own at all — it's defined entirely by the edges of the shaded regions around it.
  • Shading is a disguise. The more a figure uses filled areas, the more likely the answer is the negative (white/empty) space between them.
📐 Worked Example 2 — Harder Negative Space

Target: A triangle. Figure: A large circle with three filled circular sectors (like pie slices) leaving one triangular-looking white gap — but the gap has slightly curved sides because of the circular boundary. Trap: The gap looks like a triangle, but its sides are curved arcs, not straight lines. A pure triangle would not match.

1

Identify target's sides: The target triangle has three straight sides. Straight sides are the unique feature to hunt for.

2

Check the gap sides: Inside the circular figure, the gap's boundaries follow the circle's curved edge. These are arcs, not straight lines.

3

Conclusion: A triangle (straight sides) is NOT hidden here. The gap looks triangular but has curved boundaries. This is the "false completion" trap — don't be fooled by the overall triangular impression.

Size & Orientation Invariance

○
🌱 Simple Explanation

Imagine you cut a letter "F" out of paper. Now tilt it on its side. It's still an "F" — just rotated. Or imagine holding it in front of a magnifying glass: it looks bigger, but the shape hasn't changed. In these questions, the hidden shape might be tilted (rotated) or a different size from the target you were shown. You have to recognise the same shape even when it's been turned or scaled.

💡 Think of it like recognising your friend even when they're wearing a hat and facing a different direction.

📘 Full Explanation

Orientation invariance means the shape remains the same shape even after rotation. The angles between sides stay identical — only the direction the shape is "pointing" changes. Size invariance means the shape's proportional relationships between sides remain constant even if scaled up or down. In GL Assessment, hidden shapes are often rotated (commonly 90°, 180°, or irregular angles) or proportionally resized — the key is that relative angles and side-length ratios are preserved.

📐 Worked Example 1 — Rotation Detection

Target: An arrow pointing right (→). Figure: Contains an arrow pointing downward (↓). Is the target shape hidden?

Target: Arrow →
Figure: Rotated arrow ↓
1

Count sides of target: The right-pointing arrow has 7 sides (a notched rectangle with a triangular point). Note the right angle at the back notch.

2

Count sides of figure's shape: The downward arrow also has 7 sides. Same count. ✓

3

Check angles are preserved: The notch angle and the point angle are the same in both — just rotated 90° clockwise. The proportions of length-to-width are the same. ✓

4

Conclusion: Yes — the downward arrow IS the target shape, rotated 90°. Orientation has changed; the shape itself hasn't.

⚠️ Watch Out! — Tilt Blindness
  • "It looks different" is not a reason to reject. If you rotate the whole figure in your mind to match the target's orientation and they then match, that IS the hidden shape.
  • Mirror images are different shapes. A shape rotated is still the same shape. A shape flipped (reflected) is a different shape — like a left hand vs a right hand. GL often uses this as a trap.
  • Check the angle sizes, not the direction. Ask yourself: "Do the same angles appear in both shapes?" — not "Do they face the same way?"
📐 Worked Example 2 — Mirror Trap (Harder)

Target: A right-angled triangle with the right angle at the BOTTOM-LEFT. Figure's shape: A right-angled triangle with the right angle at the BOTTOM-RIGHT. Question: Is the figure's shape the target, just rotated?

1

Rotation test: Try rotating the target mentally. If you rotate it 180°, the right angle moves to the top-right — not the bottom-right. No rotation produces bottom-right.

2

Mirror test: If you flip the target shape left-to-right (like looking in a mirror), the right angle moves from bottom-left to bottom-right. That gives the figure's shape.

3

Conclusion: The figure's shape is the MIRROR IMAGE of the target — not the same shape. GL Assessment counts these as different. The answer is NO, the target is not hidden here. This is one of the trickiest traps in the exam.

Curve–Straight Line Hybrid Recognition

○
🌱 Simple Explanation

Some shapes are part-bendy, part-straight — like a letter "D" (one straight side, one curved side) or an ice cream cone (a triangle with a circle on top). In these questions, you need to find a shape that has both curved edges AND straight edges hidden inside a figure that's full of both wiggly lines and ruler-straight lines. The tricky part is that a curved line might blend in with nearby straight lines and you might not notice the mix.

💡 Imagine spotting a "D" letter hidden in a page of geometric shapes — one edge is a curve, one is a straight line.

📘 Full Explanation

A curve-line hybrid shape has a boundary formed by both straight line segments and curved arcs. Examples include semicircles, D-shapes, stadium shapes (rectangle with semicircular ends), or any polygon with one or more arc-shaped sides. In complex figures, straight lines and curves often intersect, making it difficult to distinguish which parts of a boundary are truly curved versus slightly angled straight lines. A key diagnostic: run your eye along each suspected side — does it bend at all, or is it perfectly straight?

📐 Worked Example 1 — Semicircle Hybrid

Target: A semicircle (one straight edge + one curved arc). Figure: Contains a large circle with a diameter line drawn across the middle and several chord lines inside.

Target: Semicircle
Figure (hidden semicircle in gold)
1

Unique feature: The semicircle has ONE straight edge and ONE perfectly curved edge. Hunt for a straight diameter line in the figure. Found: the horizontal line across the circle's centre.

2

Find the curve: Above the diameter line, the circle's top arc is a perfect semicircle. The diameter line + the top arc together form the target shape exactly.

3

Ignore extra lines: The vertical and diagonal chord lines inside the circle are distractors — they don't belong to the hidden semicircle.

⚠️ Watch Out!
  • Don't mistake a slightly curved line for straight. In busy figures, curved arcs can look almost straight if they're part of a large circle. Check carefully whether each edge bends or not.
  • The straight side of a hybrid shape must be truly straight. Not a very gradual curve — a perfectly straight edge. Trace it with your eye from end to end.
📐 Worked Example 2 — D-Shape in Complex Figure

Target: A D-shape (vertical straight edge on left, outward-curving arc on right). Figure: A rectangle with semicircles on BOTH shorter ends (a "stadium" shape), filled with crossing lines. Trap: There are two D-shapes — one on each end — but only one faces the correct direction.

1

Unique feature hunt: The D-shape has its straight side on the LEFT and the curve on the RIGHT. This direction matters — the D-shape is NOT symmetrical.

2

Check both D-shapes in the stadium: Left end = D facing right (curve on right) ✓ matches target. Right end = D facing left (curve on left) ✗ this is the mirror image.

3

Eliminate the mirror: Only the left D-shape matches. A D-facing-left is a mirror image — a different shape (like a reversed C). Answer: the target D-shape is hidden at the LEFT end of the stadium figure.

Micro-Angle Discrimination

○
🌱 Simple Explanation

Imagine two triangles that look almost identical — but one has a really sharp pointy corner and the other has a slightly less sharp corner. They look the same at first glance, but they're actually different. Micro-angle discrimination means noticing small differences in how wide or narrow corners are. A perfectly square corner (90°) is different from a nearly-square corner (85°), even though your eye might not notice immediately.

💡 Like telling the difference between two similar-sounding musical notes — C and C-sharp sound close but they're different.

📘 Full Explanation

Micro-angle discrimination requires detecting small but meaningful differences in the angles of a shape. A right angle is exactly 90°; an acute angle is less than 90°; an obtuse angle is more than 90°. GL Assessment often creates distractor options where shapes look similar to the target but have one angle slightly modified — e.g., the target has a 90° corner but the distractor has an 85° or 95° corner. The key strategy is to focus on the most extreme or unusual angle in the target shape — either the sharpest or the most obtuse — and verify it precisely in the figure.

📐 Worked Example 1 — Spotting the Off Angle

Target: A quadrilateral with one perfect right angle (exactly 90°) and three other angles. Figure options: Shape A has a slightly obtuse angle (~95°) at that corner. Shape B has exactly 90°. Shape C has a slightly acute angle (~85°).

1

Unique feature: The target has a perfect right angle (90°). A right angle is your "hunting marker" — look for the small square corner symbol.

2

Fast elimination: Shape A: the corner is slightly open (obtuse) — rejected. Shape C: the corner is slightly closed (acute) — rejected. Shape B: corner is exactly square — ✓ survives.

3

Confirm B: Trace all remaining sides. All match. Shape B is the correct hidden shape.

⚠️ Watch Out!
  • "Close enough" is never close enough. 89° is NOT a right angle in GL Assessment. Shapes that are nearly correct are the hardest traps because they feel right.
  • Rotate mentally first, then check angles. An angle's SIZE doesn't change when a shape is rotated — only its direction does. So a 90° corner is still 90° even if the shape has been turned.
📐 Worked Example 2 — The Nearly-Right-Angle Trap

Scenario: You've found a shape in the figure. It looks very similar to the target. You check the "right angle" corner — but instead of the lines meeting at exactly 90°, one line tilts ever so slightly, making the angle about 88°. The figure is busy and you might miss this. What do you do?

1

Don't accept it yet. If the target has a right angle and your found shape doesn't, this is NOT the correct match — no matter how similar everything else looks.

2

Check other positions. Look elsewhere in the figure. Is there another location where the angle is truly 90°?

3

This is the most common Mastery-level trap in GL. The 88° shape is designed to make you rush. Taking 3 extra seconds to verify the angle saves the question.

Fragmented Perimeter Detection

○
🌱 Simple Explanation

Imagine a square drawn in pencil — but someone has rubbed out part of each side, so the square looks like it's made of four short dashes instead of four solid lines. It's still a square! The sides are broken or interrupted, but the shape is still there. In these questions, the hidden shape's boundary has gaps — some lines are "broken" — but the shape is still valid if you can see where the full boundary would be.

💡 Like reading a word where some letters are faded — you can still read "h_llo" as "hello" because the structure is clear.

📘 Full Explanation

A fragmented perimeter occurs when the boundary (outline) of the hidden shape is not a continuous drawn line, but instead is made up of disconnected segments. This happens when other elements of the figure overlap the hidden shape's edges, breaking them into sections. The student must mentally "connect" these fragments to recognise the full shape. The critical rule: each fragment must lie along the correct trajectory — if you were to extend each fragment, it would align with the adjacent fragment at the correct angle.

📐 Worked Example 1 — Broken Square

Target: A square. Figure: A square with diagonal lines crossing through it, breaking its sides into short segments at each crossing point. Students often reject this because "the sides aren't continuous."

The square (gold dashes) is intact — the crossing diagonals just create intersections on the sides.

1

Don't be fooled by crossings. Where a diagonal crosses the square's side, it creates an intersection — not a gap. The square's side continues on both sides of the intersection.

2

Trace each side with your eye: Start at a corner. Move along the top edge. At each crossing, the horizontal line continues — the diagonal is just passing through. The top side is a continuous straight line from corner to corner. ✓

3

Check all four sides: Each side is a complete, straight segment from corner to corner. The square IS fully present in the figure. Correct answer: YES.

⚠️ Watch Out!
  • Crossing ≠ breaking. When two lines cross, neither line is "broken" — they each continue straight through the intersection. Students who see a crossing point and think "the side stops here" make the most common error in this sub-skill.
  • Actual gaps vs perceived gaps. A real gap means there is genuinely no line connecting two points. Don't mentally "fill in" a genuine gap — that would be the phantom line error.
📐 Worked Example 2 — The Real Gap Trap

Target: A complete triangle. Figure: Three line segments that look like they almost form a triangle — but on careful inspection, there is a genuine tiny gap at one vertex (corner) where two lines nearly meet but don't quite touch.

1

Check every vertex carefully. A vertex is where two sides of the shape are supposed to meet. If there's a real gap at a corner, the shape is NOT present.

2

Apply the rule: Unlike crossing intersections (which don't break a line), a genuine gap at a corner means the shape's boundary is incomplete. The target triangle is NOT hidden here — this is the "false completion" trap.

3

Key distinction: Intersection on a side = allowed (line continues). Gap at a vertex = not allowed (shape is incomplete). Master this distinction and you master fragmented perimeter questions.

🏆 Mastery Final Test

⏱ 12:00
Q1 Starter · Line Extension

Every side of a hidden shape must follow a real line in the figure. If one side of the hidden shape uses a line you've imagined (not actually drawn in the figure), what is this called?

✅ Correct! Using a line that isn't actually in the figure is called a "phantom line error" — one of the most common mistakes in hidden shape questions. Every single side must follow a real drawn line in the figure.
Q2 Developing · Negative Space

A figure shows a large dark cross (+) shape. What shape does the white negative space in each of the four outer corners form?

✅ Correct! A cross shape has four rectangular arms. The negative space in each of the four outer corners is a rectangle (or square if the arms are equal width), formed by the inner edges of two adjacent arms meeting at a right angle.
Q3 Starter · Orientation

A target shape is a right-pointing arrow. You find a downward-pointing arrow in the figure. Is the hidden shape present?

✅ Correct! Rotation is permitted in hidden shape questions. A downward arrow is a rightward arrow rotated 90° clockwise — the same shape, different orientation. All angles and side ratios are preserved by rotation.
Q4 Developing · Line Extension

A figure contains a large star shape formed by 5 triangles arranged around a central pentagon. How many hidden triangles are present in this figure?

✅ Correct! A 5-pointed star has exactly 5 spike triangles (one at each point). These are the direct triangular regions formed at each point of the star between two extended sides. The central pentagon is not a triangle.
Q5 Secure · Micro-Angle

The target has angles 50°-60°-70°. A shape in the figure has been rotated 180° and has angles 50°-60°-70°. Another shape in the figure (not rotated) has angles 50°-65°-65°. Which contains the hidden target?

✅ Correct! The first shape has exactly the right angles (50-60-70) just rotated — rotation never changes angle sizes, only direction. The second shape (50-65-65) has two equal angles making it isosceles, which is a completely different triangle from the scalene 50-60-70 target.
Q6 Secure · Curve-Line Hybrid

The target is a shape with two straight parallel sides and two curved ends (a "stadium" shape). A figure shows an ellipse (an oval). Is the target hidden in the figure?

✅ Correct! The stadium (hybrid) shape must have two perfectly straight parallel sides connecting two semicircular ends. An ellipse is entirely curved with no straight sections at all — it cannot contain the target shape.
Q7 Developing · Fragmented Perimeter

A hidden triangle's three sides each have other lines crossing through them (creating intersection points on each side). The triangle itself has no gaps. A student says the triangle is broken into pieces. Are they right?

✅ Correct! Intersection points on a shape's side don't break it — the line continues on both sides of the crossing. The triangle's perimeter is intact. This is the core concept of fragmented perimeter: "crossing ≠ breaking."
Q8 Mastery · Orientation

Target: an irregular triangle with angles 35°-75°-70°. In the figure, you find a triangle with angles 35°-75°-70° but it is a MIRROR IMAGE of the target (reflected, not rotated). Does the hidden shape match the target?

✅ Correct! In GL Assessment hidden shapes, reflected (mirror image) shapes count as different from the original. Think of left hand vs right hand — same angles, but genuinely different. This is especially tricky with irregular shapes where students confuse rotation with reflection.
Q9 Secure · Negative Space

A figure shows three overlapping dark circles arranged in a triangle formation. The white gap in the centre of where all three circles overlap is roughly triangular. The target is an equilateral triangle (straight sides). Is the target hidden in the white gap?

✅ Correct! Three overlapping circles create a Reuleaux-triangle-like gap in the centre, but its three sides follow the circles' curved boundaries — they curve inward (concave arcs). An equilateral triangle has three straight sides. The gap's sides are NOT straight, so it does not match the target.
Q10 Mastery · Line Extension

MASTERY: The target is a parallelogram (opposite sides parallel and equal). A figure contains four lines: two parallel horizontal lines and two parallel diagonal lines creating a grid-like pattern. How many hidden parallelograms can be found?

✅ Correct! Two parallel horizontal lines crossed by two parallel diagonal lines creates three parallelograms: one in each of the two sections between the diagonals (the smaller unit parallelograms), plus one large outer parallelogram using the full horizontal width. This type of multi-shape counting is a classic GL line extension challenge.
Q11 Secure · Fragmented Perimeter

What is the correct approach when you think you've found the hidden shape but you notice one of its corners looks like it might have a tiny gap (the two sides nearly meet but perhaps don't quite touch)?

✅ Correct! Genuine gaps at corners mean the shape's boundary is incomplete — you must reject that placement. The key is distinguishing a genuine gap from a crossing intersection. If you can confirm the two lines actually meet at that point (no gap), accept it. If there's truly no line connecting the two points, it's an incomplete boundary.
Q12 Mastery · Curve-Line

Target: a D-shape with straight side on left. In a figure, you spot a shape that has one straight side and one curved side — but the curve is concave (bends inward) rather than convex (bends outward like a D). Does it match?

✅ Correct! The direction of curvature (convex vs concave) fundamentally changes the shape. A D-shape curves outward — its arc is convex. A shape that curves inward (concave arc) is completely different, like the difference between a bowl and a dome. This is a key discriminator for hybrid shapes.
Q13 Developing · Size & Orientation

The target shape is half the size of a shape found in the figure. Both have the same angles and the same ratios between their sides. Is the target hidden in the figure?

✅ Correct! Size invariance is a key principle: the hidden shape can be scaled up or down from the target. What matters is that all angles are preserved and side-length ratios are maintained. A triangle that's twice as big but with identical angles and proportions IS the same shape.
Q14 Mastery · Negative Space + Micro-Angle

HARDEST: A figure shows four dark right-angled triangles arranged around a central point, all pointing outward. The white negative space in the very centre looks like a square. The target is a rectangle (not a square). Could the target rectangle be hidden in the negative space?

✅ Correct! This is a precision question. A square is a special rectangle (all sides equal, all angles 90°). If the target rectangle is also a square, the square gap matches. But if the target rectangle has unequal sides (e.g., 2:1 ratio), then a square gap (1:1 ratio) does NOT match — the proportions differ. Always check side ratios, not just angle types.
Q15 Mastery · All Skills

FINAL QUESTION: A student uses the "fast GL strategy." They identify one unique feature of the target (a right angle), eliminate 3 of 4 options quickly, then carefully trace the remaining option's full perimeter. They confirm all sides use real lines and all angles match. They check it's not a mirror image. What should they do LAST before marking their answer?

✅ Correct! The final verification step is the phantom line check + vertex check: confirm every edge follows a real drawn line (no imaginary lines), and every corner is at a point where lines genuinely meet or end. Once confirmed, commit. Don't over-check — you've used the correct systematic strategy.
