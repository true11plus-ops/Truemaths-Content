Sub-Skill 1: Basic Overlap Logic

🟢 Simple Explanation (Age 9)

Imagine two hula hoops lying on the floor. Where they cross over in the middle — that's the overlap. A dot placed in that middle bit means someone is in both hula hoops at the same time. A dot placed only inside one hoop means they're only in that hoop. Your job is to find which answer picture puts the dot in exactly the right place — not too many shapes, not too few!

📘 Full Explanation

In NVR "Dots in Shapes" questions, you are shown a reference diagram containing two or more overlapping shapes with one or more dots. You must identify the logical region each dot occupies — defined by which shapes it is inside, and which shapes it is outside. You then select the answer option where every dot sits in its correct corresponding region, regardless of the shapes' sizes, colours, or positions on the page.

Key rule: Only the logic (which shapes contain the dot) matters — not the visual appearance of the shapes.

🔷 Worked Example — Basic

A circle and a square overlap. A dot sits in the overlapping region (inside both shapes).
Which answer is correct?

1 Read the dot's region: The dot in the reference is inside the square AND inside the circle — it sits in the overlap.
2 Translate to a rule: I need an answer where the dot is inside BOTH shapes at the same time.
3 Check each option: A → dot is in overlap ✓ | B → dot is outside both ✗ | C → dot is in circle only ✗
4 Answer: A — the only option where the dot correctly sits inside both shapes simultaneously.
⚠️ Watch Out!

Most common mistake: Students match the visual position of the dot on the page (e.g., "it was on the left, so I pick the left one") instead of identifying the logical region. Always ask "which shapes contain this dot?" — not "where on the page is the dot?"

🔷 Worked Example — Harder

A triangle and a circle overlap. One dot is inside the triangle only. A second dot is outside both shapes. Find the correct answer.

1 Dot 1 rule: Must be inside the triangle, AND outside the circle.
2 Dot 2 rule: Must be outside the triangle AND outside the circle.
3 Elimination: Any option where either dot violates its rule is discarded immediately. Only the option satisfying BOTH rules survives.
4 Key insight: When checking Dot 2 (outside both), make sure the dot isn't accidentally sitting on a border line — boundary dots are traps!

Sub-Skill 2: Multiple Dots Strategy

🟢 Simple Explanation (Age 9)

Now there are two or three dots, each with their own rule. Think of each dot as a different person with their own invitation. One person is only allowed in the blue room. Another is only in the red room. Another is allowed in both. Your job is to find the answer that gets everyone to the right room at the same time — satisfy all the invitations together, not just one!

📘 Full Explanation

When a question contains multiple dots, each dot defines an independent logical constraint. The correct answer must satisfy every dot simultaneously. The recommended strategy is to start with the most restrictive dot — the one with the most conditions (e.g., inside three shapes) — because it eliminates the most answer options instantly. Then apply remaining dots to the survivors.

Efficiency rule: Never check all dots equally. Start with the dot that has the most shape conditions. That dot does the most work.

🔷 Worked Example — Basic (Two Dots)

Reference shows: Circle + Square overlapping. Dot A = inside circle only. Dot B = inside both shapes.

1 Most restrictive dot? Dot B (inside two shapes) — check this first. Eliminate options where Dot B is not in the overlap.
2 Survivors: Only options with Dot B correctly placed remain. Now check Dot A — it must be inside circle only (not in the overlap, not outside).
3 Final answer: Only one option places Dot A in circle-only AND Dot B in overlap simultaneously. That is correct.
4 Trap to avoid: An option where Dot A accidentally sits in the overlap — it looks close but violates the "only" condition.
🔷 Worked Example — Harder (Three Dots)

Three shapes: Circle, Square, Triangle. Dot 1 = inside Circle + Triangle only (not Square). Dot 2 = inside Square only. Dot 3 = outside all three shapes.

1 Most restrictive: Dot 1 (inside 2 shapes, outside 1) — start here. Eliminates most options.
2 Check Dot 2: Must be in square-only region. Any option with Dot 2 touching circle or triangle is wrong.
3 Check Dot 3: Must be completely outside every shape. If Dot 3 is touching any border, it fails.
4 Answer: Only one option survives all three checks. The others fail at different stages — they're designed to catch students who only check one or two dots.
⚠️ Watch Out!

Most common mistake: Checking only the first dot and selecting the first option that satisfies it — without checking the remaining dots. In GL exams, options are deliberately designed so that one option satisfies Dot 1 but fails Dot 2. Always check every dot before selecting your answer.

Sub-Skill 3: Nested Shape Logic

🟢 Simple Explanation (Age 9)

Sometimes one shape is completely inside another — like a small box sitting inside a big box. This is different from two shapes crossing over each other. If the small box is entirely inside the big box, then the region "inside both" is the entire inside of the small box. A dot inside the small box is inside BOTH boxes — even though there's no crossing over!

📘 Full Explanation

When one shape is entirely contained within another (nested), there is no traditional intersection region — instead, the inner shape's entire interior is a subset of the outer shape. Key regions become: (1) inside the inner shape only (which also means inside the outer shape — these are the same region), (2) inside the outer shape but outside the inner shape (the "ring" region), and (3) outside the outer shape entirely. Students who expect a visible crossing-over region will be tricked.

The test: Ask "Is this dot inside the outer boundary? Is it also inside the inner boundary?" Answer both questions independently.

🔷 Worked Example — Basic (Nested)
1 Identify nesting: The circle is completely inside the square. There is no crossing.
2 Dot's region: Dot is inside the circle → it is automatically also inside the square → it is "inside both".
3 Find correct answer: Look for an option with a small shape completely inside a larger shape, with the dot inside the smaller shape.
4 Trap: An option where the two shapes overlap (cross) instead of nest looks similar but is logically different!
🔷 Worked Example — Harder (Ring Region)

Large triangle contains a small circle. Dot is inside the triangle but outside the circle (the "ring" region).

1 Dot's region: Inside triangle ✓, Outside circle ✓ → The "ring" between the two shapes.
2 Rule: Correct answer must show a small shape inside a large shape, with the dot in the gap between them (inside outer, outside inner).
3 Trap option: Dot placed inside the small circle → wrong (it would be inside both, not just the triangle).
4 Another trap: Shapes that overlap instead of nesting — looks like it could work visually, but the regions are completely different logically.
⚠️ Watch Out!

Most common mistake: Confusing "nested" with "overlapping." If one shape is fully inside another, there is no separate overlap region — the entire interior of the inner shape is already inside the outer shape. Don't look for a crossing-over section that isn't there.

Sub-Skill 4: Elite Traps & High-Density Regions

🟢 Simple Explanation (Age 9)

Some questions try to trick you by making two answers look almost the same. One tiny difference — like one shape barely touching another — changes everything. The trick is to slow down and count exactly which shapes each dot is inside. Don't trust your eyes. Count carefully like you're checking a shopping list.

📘 Full Explanation

Elite GL traps include: (1) Almost-overlap — shapes that appear to touch but do not actually intersect, meaning the expected overlap region doesn't exist. (2) Extra shape intrusion — a dot satisfies two conditions correctly but accidentally sits inside a third shape it should be outside. (3) High-density regions — four or more shapes creating 6–10 logical regions; the correct region requires tracking every shape simultaneously. (4) Duplicate valid regions — the same logical region appears in two separate places in one answer option (both are valid, but students reject the answer because they expect the dot to be "in the same place" as the reference).

🔷 Worked Example — Almost-Overlap Trap

Reference: Two shapes that appear to touch at the edge — they do NOT overlap. Dot is outside both shapes but placed right next to the touching point.

1 Key check: Are these shapes actually overlapping, or just touching? If just touching, there is no overlap region.
2 Dot's region: Even though the dot is visually close to the shapes, it is outside both → look for an answer with the dot outside both shapes.
3 Trap option: An option where the shapes do properly overlap and the dot is in the overlap — this looks similar but is logically wrong (the reference has no overlap).
4 Fix: Always confirm whether shapes actually cross each other, or merely touch at an edge, before deciding what regions exist.
🔷 Worked Example — Four-Shape Nightmare

Shapes: Circle, Square, Triangle, Pentagon. Dot 1 = inside Circle + Triangle only. Dot 2 = inside Square + Pentagon only.

1 List Dot 1's conditions: Inside Circle ✓, Inside Triangle ✓, Outside Square ✓, Outside Pentagon ✓
2 List Dot 2's conditions: Inside Square ✓, Inside Pentagon ✓, Outside Circle ✓, Outside Triangle ✓
3 Use Dot 1 to eliminate: Discard any option where Dot 1 touches Square or Pentagon — most options fail here.
4 Apply Dot 2 to survivors: Now check Dot 2 is in Square+Pentagon and outside Circle+Triangle. Usually only one option survives both checks.
⚠️ Watch Out!

Most common mistake: In 4-shape questions, students stop checking conditions after confirming the "inside" shapes. Always also confirm the dot is outside the shapes it should be outside. The trap option always gets the "inside" conditions right but sneaks in an extra accidental overlap.
