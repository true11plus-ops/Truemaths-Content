Combining Shapes

Given separate pieces, identify which larger shape they combine to make — with no gaps, no overlaps, and exact edge matching.

What is this question type? You are shown two or three separate puzzle pieces. You must decide which answer option shows what they look like when fitted together — with no gaps between them, no overlapping, and every edge matching exactly.

🎯 The Fast GL Strategy — Win in 30 Seconds

1️⃣Outline-first elimination. Before touching the pieces, look at ALL answer options and ask: "Does this outline have the right total area? Does the overall shape look plausible?" Eliminate obviously wrong outlines instantly — this cuts 2–3 options in under 5 seconds.
2️⃣Limit to 2 survivors. Don't try to test all options — stop when you have 2 plausible ones. Now you're comparing just two shapes, which is far faster than scanning four.
3️⃣Anchor the largest piece. Mentally place the biggest piece in a fixed position inside your surviving options. Does the remaining space fit the other piece(s) exactly?
4️⃣Check edges, not just outline. The correct answer must have every edge matching — including internal seams where pieces meet. A correct outline with a wrong internal structure is still wrong.
5️⃣Verify: can it be rotated, not reflected? Rotation (turning) is allowed. Reflection (flipping like a mirror) is NOT. If a piece only fits by flipping it over, that answer is wrong.

No-Gap & No-Overlap Rule

○
🌱 Simple Explanation

Think of a jigsaw puzzle. When you place a piece correctly, it fits snugly — no holes left behind and no part sticking out over another piece. That's exactly the rule here. The pieces must fit perfectly: every gap is filled, and no piece overlaps another. If something pokes out or leaves a hole, it's wrong.

💡 Like fitting Tetris blocks — if a block leaves an empty square or goes on top of another block, it doesn't count as a valid move.

📘 Full Explanation

The no-gap, no-overlap principle states that the combined area of all pieces must equal the area of the final shape exactly. No region of the final shape may be left unfilled (gap), and no piece may extend beyond the boundary of the final shape or over another piece (overlap). This means: total area of pieces = area of final shape, and every square unit is occupied by exactly one piece.

📐 Worked Example 1 — Two Squares Make a Rectangle

Pieces: Two identical squares (same size). Question: Which shape do they make?

Square 1
+
Square 2
=
Rectangle ✓
1

Count area: Each square has the same area. Two squares = 2× area. Look for an answer option with exactly 2× the area of one square. A rectangle (2:1 ratio) fits perfectly. A bigger square would also have 2× area — but only if the sides work out exactly, which a 2:1 rectangle does.

2

Check for gaps/overlaps: Placed side by side, the two squares share one full edge. No space is left empty. Neither square overlaps the other. ✓

3

Trap option — the "thicker square": An option showing a slightly larger single square would look tempting if you don't check that two squares placed side-by-side can't make a square (they'd make a 2:1 rectangle). Reject it.

⚠️ Watch Out!
  • Overlap illusion: Options sometimes show pieces that would need to overlap to fit. The outline looks right, but internally there'd be double-coverage. Always ask: "Can each piece fit without sitting on top of another?"
  • Gap illusion: A shape might look like the right answer, but be slightly too big — meaning there'd be an unfilled gap. Check that the pieces exactly fill the entire interior.
📐 Worked Example 2 — Harder: Triangle + Triangle

Pieces: Two identical right-angled triangles. Options: (A) Square (B) Rectangle (C) Parallelogram (D) Larger triangle. Trap: Multiple options look plausible.

1

Outline elimination: A larger triangle (D) would only work if both triangles are placed in the exact same orientation — but two identical right-angled triangles can make a rectangle, parallelogram, or larger triangle depending on how placed. We need to test which specific option these particular triangles produce.

2

Anchor the first piece: Place one triangle with its hypotenuse facing right. The remaining space to make a rectangle must be filled by the second triangle placed with its hypotenuse facing left. This works with no gaps. ✓ Rectangle is confirmed.

3

Why the square is wrong: A square requires both sides to be equal. Two right-angled triangles placed hypotenuse-to-hypotenuse make a rectangle — only a square if the triangles have legs equal in length AND at 45°. If the triangles are not 45-45-90°, the result is NOT a square. Check the triangle's angles first.

4

Why the parallelogram is tempting: If you placed the second triangle flipped (reflected), you'd get a parallelogram-like shape. But reflection is not allowed — you can only rotate. In this question, rotation gives a rectangle. The parallelogram requires reflection → reject it.

Rotation vs Reflection

○
🌱 Simple Explanation

Imagine picking up a piece and spinning it around like a clock hand — that's rotation. You're allowed to do this. Now imagine picking up the piece and flipping it over as if you're turning a page in a book — that's reflection. You are NOT allowed to do this. The piece must stay "face up" — you can spin it, but you can't flip it.

💡 Think of a glove. You can rotate a right-hand glove in any direction, but you can never turn it into a left-hand glove without flipping it inside out. These are different objects.

📘 Full Explanation

Rotation is a rigid transformation that turns a shape by any angle around a fixed point. It preserves all distances and angles — the shape is identical, just pointing a different direction. Reflection flips the shape across an axis, creating a mirror image. The reflected shape has the same dimensions but reversed handedness (chirality). In GL Assessment combining-shapes questions, pieces may be rotated but may not be reflected. If an answer option only works by reflecting a piece, that option is incorrect.

📐 Worked Example 1 — Spotting the Illegal Reflection

Piece: A right-angled triangle with the right angle at its BOTTOM-LEFT. Options: (A) the piece rotated 90° clockwise — right angle now at top-left. (B) the piece reflected — right angle now at BOTTOM-RIGHT.

Original
Rotated 90°CW ✓
Reflected ✗ (right angle flipped)
1

Identify the asymmetry: The original piece has its right angle at the BOTTOM-LEFT. This is the "anchor feature" — it can't move to the bottom-right without a reflection.

2

Test rotation: Rotating 90° clockwise moves the right angle to the TOP-LEFT. Rotating 180° moves it to TOP-RIGHT. Rotating 270° clockwise moves it to BOTTOM-LEFT again (back to start). None of these rotations put the right angle at BOTTOM-RIGHT.

3

Conclusion: The only way to get the right angle to BOTTOM-RIGHT is to reflect the piece. Since reflection is not allowed, option B is invalid — even if the combined shape's outline looks correct.

⚠️ Watch Out! — The Most Common Trap
  • "I just rotated it" — when you actually reflected it. Students frequently confuse a 180° rotation with a reflection. For symmetric shapes, these look the same — for asymmetric shapes, they produce different results. Always ask: "Can I get from the original to this position by spinning only?"
  • Symmetric pieces feel the same when reflected. A rectangle looks the same reflected and rotated 180°. But an irregular triangle looks different. Always check asymmetric features.
📐 Worked Example 2 — Harder: The "Rotational Symmetry Confusion" Trap

Scenario: A piece looks like a checkmark (✓). Two options appear to use this piece: Option C uses it rotated 90° clockwise. Option D uses a mirror image. They look similar because the piece has an irregular shape.

1

Find one asymmetric feature in the piece: The checkmark has a short arm (top) and a long arm (bottom-right). The angle between them is not 90° — it's about 60°. This asymmetry is the key.

2

Check option C (rotated): In option C, the short arm now points to the right and the long arm points downward-left. Spinning the original 90° clockwise achieves exactly this. No reflection needed. Option C is valid. ✓

3

Check option D (reflected): In option D, the short arm points left and the long arm points downward-right — the opposite of a 90° rotation. No rotation of the original achieves this. Only a reflection does. Option D is invalid. ✗

4

If the final shape requires option D's placement, that answer is wrong — even if everything else looks correct. The reflection trap is one of the most common GL-level errors.

Edge-Length Precision Check

○
🌱 Simple Explanation

When two puzzle pieces fit together, the edges that touch must be exactly the same length. Not almost the same — exactly the same. If you're pressing a 4 cm edge against a 5 cm edge, either there's a gap, or one piece sticks out. Neither is allowed. Always ask: "Are the touching edges the same length?"

💡 Like zipping up a jacket — the left side and right side must have exactly the same number of teeth. If one side is longer, the zip doesn't close properly and the excess fabric sticks out.

📘 Full Explanation

Edge-length precision requires that wherever two pieces share a boundary (a seam), the edges they share must be identical in length. Additionally, when a piece's edge forms part of the outer boundary of the combined shape, its length must match what the answer option shows. This means: a long edge in the final shape may be formed by two shorter edges joined end-to-end (edge fragment matching), and this is valid — provided the two shorter edges together equal exactly the length of the longer boundary.

📐 Worked Example 1 — The Near-Miss Edge Trap

Pieces: A rectangle (6 cm × 3 cm) and a square (3 cm × 3 cm). Tempting wrong answer: A rectangle 9 cm × 4 cm. Correct answer: A rectangle 9 cm × 3 cm.

1

Check shared edge: The pieces are placed end-to-end along their 3 cm edges. This works because both pieces have a 3 cm side. ✓ The seam edge matches.

2

Check the resulting shape: Combined length = 6 + 3 = 9 cm. Height = 3 cm (both pieces are 3 cm tall). Correct shape = 9 cm × 3 cm rectangle. ✓

3

Why 9 cm × 4 cm is wrong: The height of 4 cm is not produced by either piece (the rectangle is 3 cm tall, the square is 3 cm tall). A height of 4 cm would require one piece to be 4 cm tall — but none are. This is the "close enough edge" error: the 9 cm length is right, but the 4 cm height is wrong. Reject it.

⚠️ Watch Out!
  • "Close enough" edge error. GL deliberately creates options where the outline looks right but one edge is slightly longer or shorter than it should be. Always verify that shared edges are exactly equal.
  • Edge fragment trap. A long side in the answer may be made up of two shorter edges from different pieces. This is allowed — but only if the two shorter edges together sum to exactly the right total length.
📐 Worked Example 2 — Edge Fragment Matching (Harder)

Pieces: A 4 cm × 2 cm rectangle and a right-angled triangle (legs: 2 cm and 2 cm). Combined along the 2 cm side.

1

The outer boundary: The bottom of the rectangle is 4 cm. When the triangle is placed at one end (sharing the 2 cm short edge), the bottom of the triangle is also 2 cm. So the total bottom boundary is 4 cm + 2 cm = 6 cm. But wait — it's not a straight 6 cm line. The rectangle contributes 4 cm of flat bottom, then the triangle adds a 2 cm base. If placed correctly, this is a flat 6 cm bottom. ✓

2

The right side: The triangle's hypotenuse becomes the right-side slanted edge. It's not 2 cm — it's 2√2 ≈ 2.83 cm. Any answer option showing a right side of exactly 2 cm is wrong. This is the edge-fragment trap: students assume the shared edge length = the hypotenuse length, but the hypotenuse is longer.

3

Correct answer shape: A pentagon (5 sides) — 4 cm bottom-left section + 2 cm bottom-right, 2 cm left side (height of rectangle), 2 cm top, and 2√2 cm slanted right side. Any option showing a right angle on the right side is wrong — the triangle creates a slant.

Internal Line Consistency

○
🌱 Simple Explanation

When you fit puzzle pieces together, the outer shape might look right — but inside the combined shape, the dividing line (where the two pieces meet) must also be in the right place. If the seam line runs across the wrong part of the shape, or at the wrong angle, the answer is wrong even if the outside looks correct. Always check: "Where exactly would the seam be inside this shape?"

💡 Like cutting a sandwich diagonally vs straight across — the outside shape is the same rectangle, but the internal cut goes in a totally different direction. If you expected a diagonal cut and got a straight one, it's the wrong cut.

📘 Full Explanation

Internal line consistency means verifying that the seam(s) between pieces — where they touch inside the final shape — are positioned and oriented correctly. Some GL Assessment questions show answer options where the outer outline is identical but the internal division (where pieces meet) differs. The correct answer is the one where the seam's position, angle, and length match what the given pieces would produce. An answer with a correct outer shape but incorrect internal seam structure is wrong.

📐 Worked Example 1 — Same Outline, Wrong Seam

Pieces: A large right-angled triangle (cut diagonally) split into two pieces by the diagonal. Options A and B both show the same rectangle outline — but Option A shows a diagonal seam, Option B shows a vertical seam.

Option A: diagonal seam ✓
Option B: vertical seam ✗
1

The outline test passes for both. Both show a rectangle — the outline is identical. A student who only checks the outer shape would accept both. This is the "perfect outline = correct answer" fallacy.

2

Check the seam: The original pieces were formed by cutting a rectangle diagonally. The seam between the two pieces must therefore be a diagonal line. Option A shows a diagonal seam → correct ✓. Option B shows a vertical seam → this would require cutting the rectangle vertically, which is not what the original pieces look like → wrong ✗.

⚠️ Watch Out! — The "Perfect Outline" Fallacy
  • Don't stop at the outer shape. Two answer options can have identical outlines but different internal seams. The internal seam must match where the pieces actually touch.
  • GL Assessment specifically designs identical-outline traps. If two options look exactly the same from the outside, you MUST look at the internal line to choose between them.
📐 Worked Example 2 — Harder: Seam at Wrong Position

Pieces: A 6×2 rectangle and a 2×2 square. Combined into an L-shape. Two options both show an L-shape, but Option C has the square on the RIGHT of the rectangle, and Option D has the square on the LEFT.

1

Determine which is correct: Look at the pieces. The 6×2 rectangle has a 2 cm height. The 2×2 square also has a 2 cm height. To make an L-shape, the square must be placed on top of one end of the rectangle (not beside it — that would just make a longer rectangle).

2

Position matters: If the question shows the square piece on the RIGHT of the given pieces picture, the L-shape's "step" must go up on the right side. Option C (square on right) places the seam at the right end. Option D (square on left) would require moving the square to the other end — which changes the seam position. If only one orientation is shown in the question, only one answer is valid.

3

Key check: Trace the internal seam line — is it at 2 cm from the right end (Option C) or 2 cm from the left end (Option D)? Match this to where the pieces would naturally join. Whichever matches the actual piece placement is correct.

Outline-First Elimination

○
🌱 Simple Explanation

Before you even think about fitting the pieces together, take 5 seconds and look at all the answer options. Ask these quick questions about each: "Is this shape roughly the right size? Does it have the right number of sides? Does it look like it could contain the pieces at all?" You can usually eliminate 2 or 3 wrong options instantly, leaving only 1 or 2 to actually test. This saves enormous time.

💡 Like choosing which box to pack your belongings into — you wouldn't try stuffing your sofa into a shoebox. You eliminate obviously wrong sizes first, then try the remaining ones.

📘 Full Explanation

Outline-first elimination is a pre-assembly screening step. Before attempting mental assembly, examine all answer options and reject any that fail basic geometric checks: (1) incorrect total area, (2) impossible number of sides (e.g., a circle from straight-edged pieces), (3) wrong overall proportions (too tall/wide), or (4) contains a curve when all pieces have straight edges. This reduces the problem to comparing 1–2 plausible options rather than testing all four exhaustively — dramatically improving speed and accuracy under time pressure.

📐 Worked Example — Fast Elimination in Action

Pieces: One equilateral triangle (side = 4 cm) and one square (side = 4 cm). Options: (A) circle (B) large equilateral triangle (C) pentagon (5 sides, all straight) (D) irregular hexagon with a curve.

1

Eliminate by curve: Option D has a curved edge. All pieces have straight edges. Combining straight-edged pieces can NEVER produce a curve. Eliminate D instantly. ✗

2

Eliminate the circle: Option A is a circle. A circle has no straight edges. Same reason as D. Eliminate A instantly. ✗

3

Eliminate by side count: One equilateral triangle (3 sides) + one square (4 sides) combined can produce at most 3+4−2 = 5 sides (when one full edge is shared between them) or more if only a partial edge matches. A large equilateral triangle (B) has only 3 sides — plausible but requires checking. A pentagon (C) has 5 sides — also plausible.

4

Now test the 2 survivors: For option B (large triangle): the triangle and square share a 4 cm edge. A square attached to one side of a triangle doesn't produce a larger triangle — it creates a 5-sided shape (pentagon). Eliminate B. ✗ Option C (pentagon) — try fitting: triangle on top of square, sharing one full 4 cm edge. Result: 5-sided shape with 4 right angles at the square corners and 2 angles from the triangle base. ✓ Answer: C.

⚠️ Watch Out!
  • Don't spend more than 5 seconds on elimination. If you're still unsure after the quick checks, move to the 2-survivor testing stage. Over-thinking the elimination step wastes the time you saved.
  • Area check first — it's instant. Count the total area of the pieces (even roughly). If an option's shape is clearly much bigger or smaller than the sum of the pieces, it's gone in 1 second.

Placement Order & Ambiguous Fit

○
🌱 Simple Explanation

Sometimes the trickiest puzzles have a piece that seems to fit in two different places — but only one of those placements leaves room for the remaining pieces to also fit. This is the "ambiguous fit" problem. The rule is: a piece placement is only correct if ALL remaining pieces can also fit perfectly afterwards. If placing a piece in one spot blocks the others from fitting, try placing it in the other spot instead.

💡 Like putting luggage in a car boot — you have to think ahead. If you put the biggest suitcase in first in the wrong spot, the smaller bags won't fit. The right order matters.

📘 Full Explanation

Placement order awareness means recognising that in multi-piece combinations, the position of one piece constrains where others can go. A piece with an ambiguous fit (it can validly occupy multiple positions in the outline) must be placed in the position that allows all remaining pieces to fill the remaining space. This is tested in GL Assessment through multi-fit ambiguity questions — where the triangle or irregular piece fits in two locations, but only one location allows the remaining piece(s) to fit without gaps or reflections.

📐 Worked Example 1 — The Triangle That Fits Twice

Pieces: One trapezium, one right-angled triangle, one small rectangle. Target shape: Irregular hexagon. The triangle appears to fit in either the top-left OR top-right section of the hexagon.

1

Test Triangle Placement 1 (top-left): Place the triangle at the top-left. The remaining space should be filled by the trapezium and rectangle. Check: does the trapezium fit in the remaining shape? Yes — the trapezium's slanted side matches the triangle's hypotenuse. The rectangle fits the remaining bottom section. ✓ Placement 1 works.

2

Test Triangle Placement 2 (top-right): Place the triangle at the top-right. Now the remaining space for the trapezium is on the left. The trapezium's slanted side faces the wrong direction — it would need to be reflected to fit. Reflection is not allowed. ✗ Placement 2 fails.

3

Conclusion: The correct answer is the option showing Placement 1 (triangle at top-left). The "forcing the last piece" trap occurs when students lock in Placement 2 and then try to make the trapezium fit by ignoring the reflection requirement.

⚠️ Watch Out! — Forcing the Last Piece
  • "Forcing the last piece" error: If you placed the first piece wrongly, the remaining space won't perfectly accommodate the other pieces. Students sometimes try to force the remaining pieces to fit anyway, ignoring small gaps or accepting a reflection. If you're forcing — go back and try a different first placement.
  • Multi-fit ambiguity is a designed trap. GL Assessment creates questions where the most "obvious" first placement is the wrong one. Always ask: "If I place this piece here, can everything else ALSO fit — without reflection, gaps, or overlap?"
📐 Worked Example 2 — Hardest: Three Pieces, One Valid Order

Pieces: Large irregular pentagon, right-angled triangle (small), small square. Target: A specific rectangular shape. Trap: the small square fits in two corners, but only one corner placement allows the triangle to slot in without a gap.

1

Start with the largest piece: Anchor the large pentagon. It fills most of the rectangle except for an L-shaped gap in one corner.

2

Test the triangle first: The triangle has a specific hypotenuse angle. Place it in the L-gap. Does it fill a triangular portion of the L-gap, leaving a rectangular space for the square? Yes → then place the square in the remaining rectangular gap. ✓ All three pieces fit in this order.

3

Test the square first instead: Place the square in the L-gap first. The remaining L-gap minus the square is an irregular shape — not a simple triangle. The triangle piece has a specific hypotenuse angle that doesn't match this irregular remaining space. ✗ Wrong order forces an impossible fit.

4

Lesson: When you're stuck, try a different placement order. The correct order is usually: largest piece first → asymmetric/angled piece second → simplest piece last.

✅ Correct! The letter "L" is asymmetric. Rotating it 90°, 180°, or 270° moves the long arm to point up, left-and-upside-down, or down — but NOT to the left while maintaining the L shape's structure. Only a reflection (forbidden in this question type) produces an arm pointing left in the mirror-image orientation. This is why reflection traps are so effective.
Q3Starter · Edge

A 4 cm × 2 cm rectangle and a 2 cm × 2 cm square are placed end-to-end along a 2 cm edge. What are the dimensions of the combined shape?

✅ Correct! Placed end-to-end along the 2 cm edges: total length = 4 + 2 = 6 cm; height = 2 cm (both pieces share the same height). Result: 6 cm × 2 cm rectangle.
Q4Secure · Internal Line

Two answer options both show the same rectangular outline. Option A has a diagonal seam; Option B has a vertical seam at the midpoint. The pieces are two identical right-angled triangles. Which is correct?

✅ Correct! The internal seam of the combined shape must match the actual boundary where the pieces touch. Two right-angled triangles joined at their hypotenuses produce a rectangle with a diagonal internal seam — not a vertical one. The "perfect outline" fallacy would lead you to pick either option; checking the seam reveals Option A is correct.
Q5Starter · Elimination

All pieces have straight edges only. Which answer option can be IMMEDIATELY eliminated?

✅ Correct! An oval is entirely curved. Straight-edged pieces cannot produce any curved boundary line. This is the fastest elimination rule: curved result from straight pieces = impossible. Eliminate the oval in under 2 seconds.
Q6Developing · Placement

In a 3-piece combination, the triangle fits in Position A or B. Position A allows all remaining pieces to fit with rotations only. Position B requires one piece to be reflected. Which is correct?

✅ Correct! Reflection is not allowed. If Position B forces any piece to be reflected, Position B is invalid — regardless of how well the shape seems to fit visually. Position A (rotation only) is the correct placement.
Q7Mastery · Rotation

MASTERY: A piece is a scalene triangle (all three sides different lengths, all three angles different). A student says: "I can use this piece in any orientation by rotating or reflecting it." Are they correct?

✅ Correct! A scalene triangle (all sides and angles different) has NO symmetry, meaning rotation and reflection produce genuinely different results. You have 3 valid rotational placements (approximately 0°, 120°, 240° — or any rotation) but 3 additional reflected orientations that are NOT valid. The lack of symmetry means the reflected versions are always distinct from the rotated ones.
Q8Secure · Edge

An answer option shows the right side of the combined shape as 5 cm. The piece on the right is a right-angled triangle with legs of 3 cm and 4 cm, with its hypotenuse forming the right side. Is 5 cm correct?

✅ Correct! Pythagoras' theorem: hypotenuse² = 3² + 4² = 9 + 16 = 25, so hypotenuse = √25 = 5 cm. The 3-4-5 triangle is a classic right-angled triangle worth memorising. Options B and C show common arithmetic errors — adding the legs or averaging them — both wrong.
Q9Developing · No-Gap

Two pieces have a combined area of 20 cm². An answer option shows a shape with area 25 cm². Can this be the correct answer?

✅ Correct! Conservation of area is absolute. 20 cm² of pieces filling a 25 cm² shape would leave 5 cm² unfilled — a gap. This violates the no-gap rule. Rotating pieces differently doesn't change their area. Any answer option with a larger total area than the sum of the pieces is wrong.
Q10Mastery · Internal + Placement

MASTERY: Options A, B, C, D all show the same hexagonal outline. A has two diagonal seams; B has two horizontal seams; C has one diagonal and one vertical seam; D has two vertical seams. Pieces are: one large parallelogram + two identical right-angled triangles. The triangles fit against the parallelogram's slanted sides. Which option is correct?

✅ Correct! The parallelogram has slanted (diagonal) sides. When the two triangles are placed against the parallelogram's slanted sides, the boundary between triangle and parallelogram is a diagonal line. This produces two diagonal seams in the combined hexagon — matching Option A. Options B and D assume the seams follow horizontal or vertical lines, which is impossible when the touching edges are slanted.
Q11Secure · Elimination

Pieces: a right-angled triangle (legs 3 cm and 4 cm) and a rectangle (4 cm × 3 cm). Total area = 6 + 12 = 18 cm². Which option is immediately eliminated by area check?

✅ Correct! The 5×5 square has area 25 cm². The pieces total 18 cm². A 25 cm² shape cannot be filled by 18 cm² of pieces — 7 cm² would be left as a gap. This eliminates the square instantly by area. Options A, B, and D all have area 18 cm² and survive the area check (they still need further testing to determine the actual answer).
Q12Mastery · Ambiguous Fit

MASTERY: A right-angled triangle fits in Position A and Position B of the target shape. In Position A, the rectangle fills the remaining gap (rotation only). In Position B, the rectangle also fills the remaining gap (also rotation only, no reflection). Both are fully valid. What should you do?

✅ Correct! When two placements are genuinely valid (no reflection needed in either), the answer options disambiguate: only one of the answer choices will show the seam in the correct position. Check whether the option shows the triangle's seam at the top-left or top-right and match it to the answer choices given. This is a high-level technique: the question's answer options are the final arbiter when placements are ambiguous.
Q13Developing · Edge Fragment

A long bottom edge of the combined shape is 8 cm. It is formed by two pieces: Piece 1 contributes a 5 cm section and Piece 2 contributes a 3 cm section. Is this "edge fragment matching" valid?

✅ Correct! Edge fragment matching is completely valid in combining shapes. Two pieces can each contribute a portion of an outer boundary edge, as long as the two contributions are collinear (lie in the same straight line) and their lengths sum to the correct total. 5 + 3 = 8 cm ✓. The key requirement is that the resulting outer edge is perfectly straight with no step or offset between the two sections.
Q14Mastery · All Skills

HARDEST: A student has eliminated 2 options by area, identified 2 survivors with the same outline, and now needs to choose. The survivors differ only in their diagonal seam direction. The pieces are a rectangle and an asymmetric (scalene) triangle. The triangle's hypotenuse runs top-left to bottom-right. Which seam direction should the correct answer show?

✅ Correct! The seam between two pieces is always along the edge they share. The triangle's hypotenuse is the edge it shares with the rectangle — so the seam follows the hypotenuse's direction. If the hypotenuse runs top-left to bottom-right, that is exactly the seam direction in the answer. Choosing the wrong seam direction means choosing the reflected version of the shape — which cannot be produced by rotation alone.
Q15Mastery · Full Strategy

FINAL QUESTION: After using the 5-step GL strategy, you have one answer option remaining. You check: the outline matches ✓, the area matches ✓, the seam direction matches ✓, no reflection is needed ✓. What is the final verification you should still perform?

✅ Correct! After all other checks pass, the final trap is the edge-length precision check. GL Assessment creates options where everything looks right — correct outline, correct area, correct seam direction — but the seam edge lengths don't quite match. A piece with a 5 cm edge touching a piece with a 4.5 cm edge would produce a tiny gap or misalignment. Verifying seam edge lengths is the final anti-trap step before committing.
