What Is "Find the Odd One Out"?

✓
Simple Explanation

Imagine five lunchboxes. Four of them have a sandwich, crisps, and a drink. One lunchbox is missing the drink. That's the odd one out — it breaks the rule that all the others follow.

In these questions, you get five shapes. Four shapes all share something in common. One shape is different in some way. Your job is to find which one is different — and why.

Full Explanation

You are presented with five figures. Four of them follow a common rule — they share one or more visual properties. The fifth figure (the odd one out) violates that rule in exactly one way. The rule may involve: number of sides, shape type, shading, rotation, position of elements, line type, or a combination of properties.

⚡ The Priority Scanning Strategy — Use This Every Time

When you look at five shapes and feel overwhelmed, use this order:

  1. 1
    Count sides. Is one shape a different type — e.g., four have 4 sides, one has 3?
  2. 2
    Check the main shape. Are they all circles, squares, triangles — except one?
  3. 3
    Check shading. Are four filled in (or empty) and one isn't?
  4. 4
    Check position. Is something inside, outside, top, bottom — when the others are different?
  5. 5
    Check rotation. Are four pointing the same way? Does one point a different direction?
  6. 6
    Kill bad rules fast. If your rule only works for 3 shapes, it is WRONG. A rule must fit all 4 of the "normal" shapes.
Golden Rule: If 2 or more shapes break your rule, your rule is wrong — start again with a different property.

Counting Sides & Elements

✓
Simple Explanation

Count the number of corners or dots or lines on each shape. Four shapes will have the same number. The odd one out has a different number. It's just like "one of these things is not like the others" on Sesame Street — but with shapes.

Full Explanation

Counting rules involve numerical properties: number of sides (vertices), number of dots or spots, number of internal lines, or number of shapes within a shape. This is the first property to check because it's the fastest and most reliable — your eyes can count before you've "decided" anything.

📐 Worked Example 1 — Step by Step

Question: Which shape is the odd one out?

A
B
C
D
E
  1. 1
    Count sides: A=3, B=4, C=5, D=5, E=4. No consistent number for all four... so sides isn't the rule here.
  2. 2
    Count shapes inside: A, B, C, D each have exactly ONE outline. E has TWO outlines (a square inside a square).
  3. 3
    Rule: Every shape has exactly one outline. E has two. E is the odd one out.
⚠️ Watch Out!

Hidden counting trap: Sometimes the elements are tiny (small dots, thin internal lines) and easy to miss. Always look inside shapes as well as at their outlines. A shape that looks like a plain square might have a dot hidden in the corner.

📐 Worked Example 2 — Harder Level

Question: Four shapes have 2 dots. Which is odd?

A
B
C
D
E

A=2 dots, B=2 dots, C=3 dots, D=2 dots, E=2 dots. C is the odd one out. The trap is that C's outer shape is a triangle (like one of the others), so students focus on shape type and miss the dot count.

Practice Questions

Starter
Four shapes each contain 1 dot. One shape contains 2 dots. Which shape is the odd one out? (Shapes: A=1 dot, B=1 dot, C=2 dots, D=1 dot, E=1 dot)
Developing
Each shape has a number of internal lines equal to its number of sides. One shape breaks this rule. Shapes: A=square with 4 lines, B=triangle with 3 lines, C=pentagon with 5 lines, D=triangle with 4 lines, E=square with 4 lines. Which is the odd one out?
Secure
All shapes contain a smaller shape inside. The number of sides of the inner shape equals the number of sides of the outer shape minus 1. Outer/Inner sides: A=4/3, B=5/4, C=6/5, D=3/2, E=4/3. Which is the odd one out?
Mastery
Each shape has dots, and the rule involves BOTH the outer shape's sides AND the dot count. The rule is: dots = (sides ÷ 2). Which shape breaks this rule? A=hexagon/3 dots, B=square/2 dots, C=pentagon/2 dots, D=hexagon/3 dots, E=square/2 dots

Shape Type & Category

✓
Simple Explanation

Think of sorting socks. If four socks are ankle socks and one is a knee-high sock, the knee-high one doesn't belong. In shape questions, four shapes might all have curved edges (like circles and ovals), but one has straight edges. That straight-edged one is the odd one out.

Full Explanation

Shape type rules involve the category that four shapes belong to. Common categories include: curved vs straight-sided, regular vs irregular, convex vs concave, symmetrical vs asymmetrical. The odd one out belongs to a different category even if it looks similar at first glance.

📐 Worked Example — Curved vs Straight

A=oval, B=circle, C=ellipse, D=square, E=lens shape

  1. 1
    Look at each shape: oval (curved), circle (curved), ellipse (curved), square (STRAIGHT sides), lens (curved).
  2. 2
    Rule: four shapes have only curved edges. D (the square) has four straight edges.
  3. 3
    D is the odd one out. The trap is that a square is a very familiar shape — but it belongs to the wrong category here.
⚠️ Watch Out!

The "Looks Unique" trap: One shape may look wildly different from the others — but actually follows the rule perfectly. Don't let visual weirdness fool you. A very large hexagon still follows a "6-sided shapes" rule, even if it looks nothing like the small hexagons next to it.

Practice Questions

Starter
A=triangle, B=square, C=rectangle, D=hexagon, E=circle. Four shapes have only straight sides. Which is the odd one out?
Developing
All shapes appear to be quadrilaterals (4-sided). A=square, B=rectangle, C=trapezium, D=rhombus, E=pentagon. Which is the odd one out?
Secure
The rule involves lines of symmetry. A=equilateral triangle (3 lines), B=square (4 lines), C=isosceles triangle (1 line), D=rectangle (2 lines), E=scalene triangle (0 lines). Four shapes have at least one line of symmetry. Which is the odd one out?
Mastery
The shapes look very different but four share a hidden property. A=large irregular hexagon, B=small regular hexagon, C=hexagon with one corner pushed in, D=stretched hexagon, E=pentagon. What rule do four follow, and which is the odd one out?

Shading & Fill

✓
Simple Explanation

Imagine five coloured-in pictures. Four are coloured fully black. One is just an outline with no colour inside. That empty outline is the odd one out. In shape questions, "shading" means whether a shape is filled in (black/dark), half filled, striped, or empty (just an outline).

Full Explanation

Shading rules involve: filled (solid) vs empty (outline only), partial shading (top half vs bottom half), striped patterns, and conditional shading (e.g. "if 4 sides → shaded, if 3 sides → unshaded"). These conditional rules are top-set discriminators — always check whether shading relates to shape type.

📐 Worked Example — Conditional Shading
  1. 1
    Shapes: A=filled triangle, B=filled triangle, C=empty square, D=filled triangle, E=filled square
  2. 2
    Check if 3-sided shapes are filled: A✓, B✓, D✓. Check if 4-sided shapes are empty: C✓ but E is FILLED.
  3. 3
    Rule: 3-sided = filled, 4-sided = empty. E is a 4-sided filled shape — it breaks the rule. E is the odd one out.
⚠️ Watch Out!

Partial shading trap: "Half shaded" is a specific property — top half shaded is different from bottom half shaded. Don't just check whether a shape is shaded or not — check which part is shaded. A shape with the right half shaded might look like all the others at first glance.

Practice Questions

Starter
A=filled black circle, B=filled black circle, C=empty circle (outline only), D=filled black circle, E=filled black circle. Which is the odd one out?
Developing
Each shape is half shaded. A=top half shaded, B=top half shaded, C=top half shaded, D=bottom half shaded, E=top half shaded. Which is the odd one out?
Secure
The conditional rule is: shapes with an even number of sides are shaded; shapes with an odd number of sides are empty. A=shaded square (4 sides), B=empty triangle (3 sides), C=shaded hexagon (6 sides), D=shaded pentagon (5 sides), E=empty triangle (3 sides). Which is the odd one out?
Mastery
Each shape contains a smaller inner shape. The rule is: the inner shape is shaded if and only if the outer shape is unshaded, and vice versa. A=shaded square/unshaded circle inside. B=unshaded triangle/shaded dot inside. C=shaded pentagon/unshaded star inside. D=unshaded rectangle/shaded cross inside. E=shaded hexagon/shaded circle inside. Which is the odd one out?

Rotation & Reflection

✓
Simple Explanation

Imagine an arrow. If you spin it (rotate it), it still looks like the same arrow — just pointing in a different direction. If you flip it in a mirror (reflect it), it looks like a "backwards" arrow. Rotating and reflecting are NOT the same thing. Four shapes might all be rotated versions of one shape — but one might be a reflected (flipped) version instead. That reflected one is the odd one out.

Full Explanation

Rotation: spinning the shape around its centre (90°, 180°, 270°). The shape stays the same — it's just pointing in a different direction. Reflection: flipping the shape in a mirror. An asymmetric shape reflected looks different from any rotation of the original. For shapes with a "hand" (like an L, F, or arrow), reflection creates its mirror image — which can never be achieved by rotation alone. Also watch for progressive rotation sequences: a shape rotating 90° each time (top → right → bottom → left).

📐 Worked Example — Rotation vs Reflection
  1. 1
    Five L-shapes: A points top-right, B points bottom-right, C points bottom-left, D points top-left, E is a MIRRORED L (reversed) pointing top-right.
  2. 2
    Rotate A clockwise: 90° gives B, 180° gives C, 270° gives D. So A, B, C, D are all rotations of the same L.
  3. 3
    E looks like it's in the same position as A — but the corner flips the opposite way. This is a reflection, not a rotation. E is the odd one out.
Quick test for reflection: Trace shape A on paper, flip the paper over — does it look like E? If yes, E is a reflection of A, not a rotation.
⚠️ Watch Out!

Symmetric shapes cannot be reflected differently from rotated. A square, circle, or regular hexagon looks the same after reflection as after rotation. Only asymmetric shapes (like L, F, S, arrows) can have a "reflection vs rotation" rule. If all shapes are symmetric, the rotation rule must be about their internal elements (like a dot moving around).

Practice Questions

Starter
Five arrows: A points right, B points right, C points left, D points right, E points right. Four arrows point the same way. Which is the odd one out?
Developing
Five R-shapes (like the letter R). A=normal R, B=R rotated 90° clockwise, C=R rotated 180°, D=R rotated 270° clockwise, E=mirror-image R (backwards R). Four are rotations of the same shape. Which is a reflection and therefore the odd one out?
Secure
Five squares each contain a dot that follows a clockwise rotation sequence: A=dot at top, B=dot at right, C=dot at bottom, D=dot at left, E=dot at top-right corner. Which breaks the clockwise rotation rule?
Mastery
Five squares, each with a dot AND an arrow inside. The rule is: the dot moves clockwise (top→right→bottom→left) AND the arrow always points TOWARD the dot. A=dot top/arrow up, B=dot right/arrow right, C=dot bottom/arrow down, D=dot left/arrow pointing right (NOT left), E=dot top/arrow up. Which shape has two elements breaking the rule?

Position & Location

✓
Simple Explanation

Think of a dot playing hide-and-seek. In four shapes, the dot is always inside the shape. In one shape, the dot is outside. That's the odd one out — the dot is in the wrong place. Position means: where is something? Inside or outside? Top or bottom? Left or right? Touching or not touching?

Full Explanation

Position rules involve the location of one element relative to another. This includes: inside vs outside a shape; top/bottom/left/right within a shape; touching the edge vs floating in the centre; overlapping vs separate; and cycling positions (dot moves clockwise around a shape in a sequence). Position rules are often hidden inside otherwise complex-looking figures.

📐 Worked Example — Inside vs Outside
  1. 1
    Five shapes: A=square with dot inside, B=triangle with dot inside, C=pentagon with dot inside, D=circle with dot inside, E=square with dot OUTSIDE (to the right of the shape).
  2. 2
    Rule: the dot is always inside the shape. Check each one: A✓, B✓, C✓, D✓, E✗ (dot is outside).
  3. 3
    E is the odd one out. This trains position awareness — very easy to miss if you're distracted by the different outer shapes.
⚠️ Watch Out!

"Near the edge" isn't the same as "on the edge". A dot can be close to a corner without actually touching the boundary. Be precise — touching the edge is a specific rule. Also, "top half" and "top-right corner" are different positions. Always ask yourself: exactly where is this element?

Practice Questions

Starter
Each square has a dot. A=dot inside, B=dot inside, C=dot outside, D=dot inside, E=dot inside. Which is the odd one out?
Developing
Five circles each have a small cross (+) inside. The rule is the cross is always in the TOP half of the circle. A=cross top-left, B=cross top-right, C=cross top-centre, D=cross bottom-centre, E=cross top-left. Which is the odd one out?
Secure
Five squares each have a small circle. The circles cycle clockwise: A=circle at top, B=circle at right, C=circle at bottom, D=circle at top (it should be at left), E=circle at top. One square has the circle out of sequence. Which is the odd one out?
Mastery
The rule involves TWO elements: a dot and a star inside each shape. The rule is: the dot is always in the TOP half AND the star is always in the BOTTOM half. A=dot top/star bottom ✓, B=dot top/star bottom ✓, C=dot top/star top ✗, D=dot top/star bottom ✓, E=dot bottom/star bottom ✗. Two shapes appear to break the rule. But only ONE shape breaks BOTH parts of the rule. Which is it?

Line Type & Style

✓
Simple Explanation

Lines can be solid (—), dashed (- - -), dotted (···), thick, or thin. Imagine four notebook pages all drawn with solid lines and one with dashed lines. The dashed one is different — it's the odd one out. In shape questions, always check whether the outline of the shape (and any lines inside it) are the same type as all the others.

Full Explanation

Line-type rules cover: solid vs dashed vs dotted outlines, thick vs thin borders, single vs double outlines, and combinations (e.g. dashed internal lines but solid outer border). This is one of the most commonly overlooked sub-skills — students scan shape and shading first and miss the line style entirely. Always check line type as part of your scanning routine.

⚠️ Watch Out!

Internal lines vs outer border: A shape might have a solid outer border (like all the others) but dashed internal lines — or vice versa. Check both the border AND any lines inside the shape separately. Don't assume the same line type applies everywhere in the figure.

Practice Questions

Starter
Five squares: A=solid border, B=solid border, C=dashed border, D=solid border, E=solid border. Which is the odd one out?
Developing
Five circles, each with a horizontal line through the centre. The rule is: the circle's border is solid AND the internal line is dashed. A=solid/dashed ✓, B=solid/dashed ✓, C=solid/solid ✗, D=solid/dashed ✓, E=solid/dashed ✓. Which is the odd one out?
Secure
Five shapes with double outlines (two concentric borders). The rule: the outer border is thick/solid AND the inner border is thin/solid. A=thick outer/thin inner ✓, B=thick outer/thick inner ✗, C=thick outer/thin inner ✓, D=thick outer/thin inner ✓, E=thick outer/thin inner ✓. Which is the odd one out?
Mastery
The rule combines line type AND shape type: straight-sided shapes have dashed borders; curved shapes have solid borders. A=dashed triangle ✓, B=solid circle ✓, C=dashed square ✓, D=solid oval ✓, E=dashed circle ✗. Which shape breaks the conditional line-type rule?

Correspondence & Element Links

✓
Simple Explanation

Imagine every child in a class has a coat that matches their hat — red hat, red coat; blue hat, blue coat. The rule links two things together. If one child has a red hat but a blue coat, they're the odd one out — the link is broken. In shape questions, the "link" might be: the dot colour matches the shape colour, or the number of sides matches the number of dots.

Full Explanation

Correspondence rules link two or more properties together: shading corresponds to shape type, dot count corresponds to side count, inner shape type corresponds to outer shape type, etc. These are advanced rules because you must identify the relationship between two separate properties — not just one property in isolation. Common at GL top-set level.

⚠️ Watch Out!

Dual-rule conflict trap: Sometimes two possible rules both appear valid for 3 or 4 shapes. Only one rule works perfectly for all 4 of the "correct" shapes. If your rule has an exception, it is the wrong rule — keep looking. A real rule has exactly zero exceptions among the four matching shapes.

Practice Questions

Starter
The rule: the number of dots inside each shape equals the number of sides. A=triangle/3 dots, B=square/4 dots, C=pentagon/5 dots, D=hexagon/5 dots, E=triangle/3 dots. Which is the odd one out?
Developing
The rule: if the outer shape is filled, the inner shape is empty; if the outer shape is empty, the inner shape is filled. A=filled outer/empty inner ✓, B=empty outer/filled inner ✓, C=filled outer/empty inner ✓, D=filled outer/filled inner ✗, E=empty outer/filled inner ✓. Which is the odd one out?
Secure
The rule links shape type to line style AND shading: triangles are always dashed+empty; quadrilaterals are always solid+filled. A=dashed empty triangle ✓, B=solid filled square ✓, C=solid filled rectangle ✓, D=dashed filled triangle ✗, E=solid filled rhombus ✓. Which breaks the combined rule?
Mastery
A very hard GL-style question. Each figure has an outer shape and an inner shape. Two separate rules apply simultaneously: (1) number of inner shape sides = outer shape sides minus 2; (2) if outer shape has more than 4 sides, the inner shape is shaded. A=hexagon/shaded square (6-2=4 ✓, 6>4 so shaded ✓), B=pentagon/empty triangle (5-2=3 ✓, 5>4 so should be shaded ✗), C=square/empty line/2-sided (4-2=2 ✓, 4 is NOT more than 4 so empty ✓), D=hexagon/shaded square ✓, E=pentagon/shaded triangle ✓. Which shape breaks a rule?

Common Traps & Misconceptions

✓
⚠️ Trap 1: "Looks Unique" Trap

One shape visually stands out and looks completely different — but actually follows all the rules. Students pick this shape just because it looks weird. Always verify your choice by confirming ALL four other shapes follow the same rule.

⚠️ Trap 2: "Two Odd Ones" Illusion

Two shapes look different from the others. Only one actually breaks the rule. Check each "odd looking" shape carefully — one of them might actually follow the rule when you look closely enough.

⚠️ Trap 3: Overfitting Your Rule

You create a complicated rule that fits 3 shapes — then force the 4th to fit. If your rule doesn't fit all four "normal" shapes naturally, it is WRONG. Keep it simple and keep testing.

⚠️ Trap 4: Hidden Commonality

All shapes share a subtle hidden feature you haven't noticed (e.g. all have at least one right angle). You find an obvious rule, pick your answer — but the real rule is something else entirely, and your chosen answer actually follows the real rule. Always ask: "Is there something ALL four of these share that I haven't thought about yet?"

⚠️ Trap 5: Negative Rules

The rule is what shapes do NOT have: "no shape touches another", "no lines intersect", "no shape has a right angle". Negative rules are harder to spot because your brain looks for what IS there, not what is absent. Train yourself to ask: "What is missing from this shape that the others have?"

Trap Practice Questions

Secure
"Looks Unique" Trap: Five shapes — A=tiny square, B=tiny circle, C=giant irregular blob, D=tiny triangle, E=tiny pentagon. C looks completely unlike the others. But the rule is: all shapes have at least one curved edge. Which is the odd one out?
Mastery
Negative Rule: Five shapes — each contains exactly two smaller shapes inside. The rule is: "no two inner shapes touch each other." A=two circles apart ✓, B=two squares apart ✓, C=two triangles TOUCHING ✗, D=two ovals apart ✓, E=two rectangles apart ✓. Which breaks the negative rule?
