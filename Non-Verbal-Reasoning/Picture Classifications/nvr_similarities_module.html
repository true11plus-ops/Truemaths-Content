What is "Similarities"? ✅
Simple Explanation

You get a group of shapes that all follow the same secret rules. Your job is to find what those rules are — and then pick the answer that follows ALL the same rules.

Think of it like a club with strict rules. If every member wears a hat, has spots, and faces left — then to join the club, you MUST wear a hat, have spots, AND face left. One rule wrong and you're out!
Full Explanation

In NVR Similarities questions, you are presented with a set of 5 example shapes (the "group") that all share a defined set of geometric properties. You must identify the complete rule set governing those properties — which may include shape type, quantity, shading, position, orientation, size, internal elements, line type, and relationships between elements — and then select the answer option that satisfies every identified rule simultaneously.

A common trap: distractor options satisfy most rules but fail on one. A correct answer must pass ALL rules, not just the majority.

The SPANS Strategy ✅
Simple Explanation — SPANS

SPANS is your scanning checklist. Every time you look at a group of shapes, go through each letter in order. This stops you from panicking or missing something!

It's like a pilot's pre-flight checklist. They check every single thing in order — they don't skip steps, even if they're in a hurry.
S
Shape
What type of shape is it? Circle, triangle, square, arrow, star?
P
Position
Where is it? Top, bottom, left, right, centre, corner?
A
Amount
How many shapes, dots, sides, or lines are there?
N
Nature
What type of lines? Straight, curved, dashed? Do lines touch or cross?
S
Shading
Is it filled black, white, grey, striped, or dotted?
Priority Scanning Order

Always scan in this order — it finds rules fastest and eliminates wrong answers quickly:

1
Amount — count first. It's the easiest to check and eliminates the most options instantly.
2
Shading — black/white/grey is very easy to see and often a key rule.
3
Shape — what type of shape? Is it always the same, or does the type not matter?
4
Position — where is the shape sitting? Is there always a small shape in a corner?
5
Nature — line type, rotation, whether shapes touch. Check this last — it's the trickiest.

2-Rule Minimum: If you can only find 1 rule, keep looking. GL questions almost always use at least 2 rules simultaneously. Finding only 1 means you're probably about to choose a trap answer.

⚠️ Watch Out!

The Most Common SPANS Mistake

Students stop scanning after finding the first rule. They think "all shapes are circles — done!" and pick the first circle they see in the answers. But the right answer might need to be a white circle with a dot inside in the top corner. Always finish the full SPANS scan before looking at answer options.

Shape Rules ✅
Simple Explanation

Look at ALL the shapes in the group and ask: "What kind of shape is it?" Sometimes ALL shapes are the same type (all circles). Sometimes the TYPE doesn't matter — but the number of SIDES does. Count the sides carefully!

It's like sorting your LEGO bricks. Sometimes all bricks must be the same colour AND shape. Sometimes the colour doesn't matter — only the size does.
Full Explanation

Shape rules govern the geometric form of elements. Key properties to check:

  • Shape type constant: All shapes are always circles / always triangles etc.
  • Shape type irrelevant: The type varies — the rule is about something else (e.g., number of sides > 4).
  • Inner vs outer shape: Outer shape may vary; inner shape stays fixed (or vice versa).
  • Size rule: One shape is always larger/smaller than another.
  • Symmetry: All shapes are symmetrical (or all are asymmetrical).
Worked Example — Basic Shape Rule

Group: Large circle with small triangle inside. Large square with small triangle inside. Large pentagon with small triangle inside.

1
Check Amount: Every group member has exactly 2 shapes (1 large + 1 small).
2
Check Shape of outer: Circle, square, pentagon — these VARY. So the outer shape type is NOT the rule.
3
Check Shape of inner: Triangle, triangle, triangle — ALWAYS a triangle! This IS the rule.
4
Check Shading: The inner triangle is always white (unfilled).
5
Rules found: (1) 2 shapes total, (2) inner is always a white triangle, (3) outer can be any shape.
6
Choose answer: Find the option with any large shape containing a white triangle inside. ✅
⚠️ Watch Out!

The "Number = Sides" Trap

A group shows: triangle with 1 dot, square with 1 dot, pentagon with 1 dot. Students think: "dots = sides − 2" and pick the hexagon with 4 dots. But the actual rule is simply always 1 dot, regardless of shape. Don't invent rules that aren't there!

Harder Worked Example — Inner Shape Stays Constant

Group: Pentagon containing black circle. Hexagon containing black circle. Irregular quadrilateral containing black circle.

1
Amount: Always 2 shapes (1 outer, 1 inner).
2
Outer shape: Pentagon (5 sides), Hexagon (6 sides), Quad (4 sides) — VARIES. Not the rule.
3
Inner shape: Circle, Circle, Circle — CONSTANT. Rule: inner = circle.
4
Shading of inner: Black, Black, Black — Rule: inner circle is always filled black.
5
Answer: Any outer shape containing a black filled circle. ✅ Trap: a shape with a white circle inside ❌ or a black triangle inside ❌.

Number / Amount Rules ✅
Simple Explanation

Count everything you can see: shapes, dots, lines, sides, stripes. Does the count ALWAYS stay the same? That's a rule! Check your count at least twice — rushing leads to counting errors.

Think of it like counting people in a photo. If every photo in a series has exactly 3 people, then the answer photo must also have exactly 3 people — no more, no less.
Full Explanation

Number rules cover:

  • Total shape count: Always 2, always 3, etc.
  • Dot/mark count: Number of internal or external dots/circles.
  • Side count: All shapes always have the same number of sides.
  • Line count: Number of lines inside or outside a boundary.
  • Element ratio: E.g., always twice as many dots as shapes.

Overcounting trap: Students sometimes count internal AND external elements together incorrectly — e.g., counting dots inside AND dots outside as one total, when they follow separate rules.

Worked Example

Group: Square with 3 dots inside. Pentagon with 3 dots inside. Circle with 3 dots inside.

1
Shape type: Square, Pentagon, Circle — varies. Not the rule.
2
Dot count: 3, 3, 3 — CONSTANT. Rule: always 3 dots inside.
3
Position of dots: Scattered inside each shape — no fixed position rule.
4
Answer: Any shape containing exactly 3 dots inside. A hexagon with 3 dots ✅. A hexagon with 4 dots ❌.
⚠️ Watch Out!

The Overcounting Trap

If a shape has 2 dots inside AND 1 dot outside, count them separately! The rules might be "always 2 inside, always 1 outside" — not "always 3 total." Treat internal and external elements as separate categories when checking Amount.

Harder Worked Example — Ratio Rule

Group: 1 square, 2 dots. 1 triangle, 2 dots. 1 circle, 2 dots.

1
Shape count: Always 1 outer shape.
2
Dot count: Always exactly 2 dots.
3
Key insight: Shape type VARIES, dot count CONSTANT. The two rules are independent.
4
Trap answer: A pentagon with 5 dots looks tempting if you think "dots = sides" — but the rule is always 2 dots regardless of the shape's sides.

Shading Rules ✅
Simple Explanation

Is each shape filled in (black), empty (white), grey, or patterned (stripes/dots)? Sometimes ALL shapes have the same shading. Sometimes the OUTER shape is always white but the INNER shape is always black.

Think of colouring-in: if every shape in the group has been coloured the same way, the answer must be coloured the same way too!
Full Explanation

Shading rules cover:

  • Solid black (filled): Shape is entirely black.
  • White/outline only: Shape has a border but is not filled.
  • Grey/half-shaded: Shape is partially filled.
  • Striped/hatched: Shape has diagonal lines inside.
  • Shading contrast rule: E.g., outer shape always white, inner always black (or vice versa).
  • Opposite shading: Large shape black → small shape white; always inverted.
Worked Example — Contrast Rule

Group: Black outer square, white inner circle. Black outer triangle, white inner circle. Black outer hexagon, white inner circle.

1
Outer shading: Black, Black, Black — constant. Rule: outer is always black.
2
Inner shading: White, White, White — constant. Rule: inner is always white.
3
Inner shape type: Circle, Circle, Circle — constant. Rule: inner is always a circle.
4
Answer: A black outer shape containing a white circle. ✅ Wrong: white outer with black circle ❌.
⚠️ Watch Out!

The "Almost Correct" Shading Trap

The most deceptive trap: an option that gets shape, number, and position right — but uses the WRONG shading. For example, the group uses black inner shapes, but a trap answer shows a grey inner shape. Grey and black look similar at a glance. Always name the exact shading out loud: "filled solid black" vs "grey" vs "striped."

Harder Worked Example — Opposite Shading

Group: Large black shape, small white shape. The pair is always one black and one white of the same shape type.

1
Shading check: Large = black, Small = white. Rule: shading is always OPPOSITE between large and small.
2
Shape type: Both shapes must be the same type (both circles, or both triangles).
3
Answer: A large black version + small white version of the same shape. E.g., large black pentagon + small white pentagon. ✅
4
Trap: Large white + small black (shading rule reversed) ❌.
Starter

Position Rules ✅
Simple Explanation

Where is the shape? Top-left? Centre? Bottom-right? Is there always a small dot in a corner? Does the big shape always sit on the right? Think of a 3×3 grid inside the box — which cell is the shape always in?

Imagine a 9-square grid like a noughts-and-crosses board. If the star is always in the TOP-LEFT square, then the answer must also have the star in the TOP-LEFT square.
Full Explanation

Position rules cover:

  • Absolute position: Always top-left, always centre, etc.
  • Relative position: Small shape is always above the large shape.
  • Inside/outside: Dot always inside the boundary vs always outside.
  • Edge/corner vs centre: "Element always touches a corner" or "element never touches the boundary."
  • Alignment: Two shapes always aligned horizontally/vertically.
Worked Example

Group: Each box has a large shape on the LEFT and a small dot on the RIGHT.

1
Large shape position: Always LEFT. Constant rule.
2
Small dot position: Always RIGHT. Constant rule.
3
Answer: Any large shape on the left with a small dot on the right. ✅
4
Trap: Large shape on the right with dot on the left — looks the same but is mirrored ❌.
⚠️ Watch Out!

The Mirror Trap

A trap answer will place elements in the correct position but MIRRORED (left-right flipped or top-bottom flipped). Always state the exact position: "dot is in the BOTTOM-RIGHT corner" — not just "dot is in a corner." Top-right ≠ bottom-right.

Harder Worked Example — Inside/Outside

Group: Pentagon → dot is outside to the right. Hexagon → dot is outside to the right. Circle → dot is outside to the right.

1
Dot position: Outside, to the right in all cases. Clear rule.
2
Shape type: Changes — not the rule.
3
Trap: A square with a dot INSIDE the square — correct shape type, wrong position ❌.
4
Answer: Any shape with a dot OUTSIDE to the RIGHT ✅.

Rotation & Reflection Rules ✅
Simple Explanation

Some shapes look different because they've been spun around (rotated) or flipped (reflected like in a mirror). An arrow pointing up, right, down, left — it's the same arrow, just turned! Look past the direction and check: is it the same shape?

Think of a clock hand. At 12 o'clock it points up. At 3 o'clock it points right. It's still the same hand — it's just been rotated 90 degrees each time!
Full Explanation

Rotation and reflection rules cover:

  • Rotation sequences: Shape rotates by a fixed amount each step (90°, 45°, etc.).
  • Consistent orientation: All group members show the shape at the same angle.
  • Reflection/mirror: Shape is flipped horizontally or vertically.
  • Orientation illusion: Same shape rotated looks "different" — students reject it incorrectly.
  • Rotation + shading: Shape rotates AND shading changes together (compound rule).

Key insight: When checking for rotation, mentally hold the shape and spin it — does it become a group member? If yes, it might be correct.

Worked Example — Rotation Rule

Group: Box 1: square with arrow pointing UP. Box 2: square with arrow pointing RIGHT. Box 3: square with arrow pointing DOWN.

1
Shape type: Always a square + an arrow. ✓
2
Shading: Square is always white, arrow is always black. ✓
3
Rotation pattern: UP → RIGHT → DOWN. Each step is +90° clockwise. So the next is LEFT.
4
Answer: White square with black arrow pointing LEFT. ✅
5
Trap: Arrow pointing UP again (students think it "resets") ❌. Or arrow pointing RIGHT (students miss the pattern) ❌.
⚠️ Watch Out!

The Orientation Illusion

Students often reject a rotated shape because it "looks different." An L-shape rotated 90° looks like a backwards J — but it IS the same L-shape, just rotated. Don't let rotation fool you into thinking it's a different shape. Always ask: "If I rotated this, would it look like the other group members?"

Harder Worked Example — Rotation + Shading Combined

Group: Box 1: black triangle pointing UP. Box 2: white triangle pointing RIGHT. Box 3: black triangle pointing DOWN.

1
Shape: Always a triangle.
2
Rotation: UP → RIGHT → DOWN → ? = LEFT (90° clockwise each time).
3
Shading: Black → White → Black → ? = WHITE (alternating).
4
Compound rule! Two rules working together: rotation AND alternating shading.
5
Answer: WHITE triangle pointing LEFT. ✅ Trap: black triangle pointing left ❌ (right rotation, wrong shading).

Line Relationship Rules ✅
Simple Explanation

Sometimes the rule is about lines — not shapes. Do the lines always cross? Do they always run side-by-side (parallel)? Do they always touch the edge of the box — or never touch it?

Think of train tracks. Parallel tracks never cross. But some roads cross each other at junctions. Check: do the lines cross, run alongside each other, or touch the walls of the box?
Full Explanation

Line rules include:

  • Parallel lines: Two or more lines always run in the same direction without meeting.
  • Intersecting lines: Lines always cross each other.
  • Line count: Always 2 lines, always 3 lines, etc.
  • Touching boundary: Lines always touch the edge — or lines never touch the edge.
  • Line type: Always straight, always curved, always dashed.
  • Meeting point: Lines always meet at the centre — or always meet at a corner.
Worked Example — Parallel Lines

Group: Box 1: two horizontal parallel lines. Box 2: two diagonal parallel lines. Box 3: two vertical parallel lines.

1
Line count: Always exactly 2 lines.
2
Relationship: Always parallel (never cross). Constant rule.
3
Direction: Varies (horizontal, diagonal, vertical). NOT the rule.
4
Answer: Any 2 parallel lines, regardless of direction. ✅ Two crossing lines ❌.
⚠️ Watch Out!

Near-Miss Line Traps

Two lines that are almost parallel but angle slightly toward each other may look parallel at a glance. Similarly, "lines meet at the edge" and "lines touch the corner" are different rules. Read line positions very precisely.

Harder Worked Example — Boundary Rule

Group: Every box has lines that start at the boundary edges but never intersect each other inside the box.

1
Rule 1: Lines always touch the boundary (edge of the box).
2
Rule 2: Lines inside the box never cross each other.
3
Answer: Lines touching edges, not crossing inside ✅. Lines floating in middle ❌. Lines crossing inside ❌.

Conditional & Negative Rules ✅
Simple Explanation

Some rules only apply IF something else is true. Like: "IF the outer shape is a triangle, the dot is INSIDE. IF the outer shape is a circle, the dot is OUTSIDE." You have to learn both parts of the rule!

Like a secret password that changes depending on who you are. If you're wearing a red badge, the password is "apple." If you're wearing a blue badge, the password is "banana." Both rules exist at the same time.
Full Explanation

Conditional and negative rules include:

  • Conditional pairing: "If outer = circle → inner = triangle." "If outer = square → inner = circle."
  • Negative rules: "No shapes touch." "No lines cross." "No element reaches the boundary."
  • Hidden constraint: Only ONE element changes between group members; everything else is fixed.
  • Symmetry-breaking: Shapes look symmetrical but have one tiny deliberate violation (e.g., one dot missing).
Worked Example — Conditional Pairing

Group: Triangle → dot inside. Circle → dot outside. Square → dot inside. Hexagon → dot outside.

1
Pattern: Triangle: inside. Circle: outside. Square: inside. Hexagon: outside.
2
Rule discovered: Polygons with an ODD number of sides → dot inside. Shapes with EVEN sides (or curves) → dot outside.
3
Test: Pentagon (5 sides = odd) → dot inside. Octagon (8 sides = even) → dot outside.
4
Answer: Match the conditional rule to the shape given in the answer options. ✅
⚠️ Watch Out!

The "Hidden Constraint" Trap

In some groups, ONLY ONE element changes between members — everything else is identical. Students who only notice the changing element often pick wrong answers because they ignore the fixed elements. Always ask: "What is STAYING THE SAME across all members?"

Harder Worked Example — Negative Rule

Group: Multiple shapes in each box, but shapes NEVER touch each other. They are always separated by a gap.

1
Positive rule: Multiple shapes are always present.
2
Negative rule: No two shapes ever touch each other.
3
Trap answer: Correct shapes and shadings, but two shapes are touching ❌.
4
Answer: Same shapes with clear gaps between all of them ✅.

Common Traps & Misconceptions ✅
Simple Explanation — Know Your Enemies

GL examiners are clever. They design wrong answers that look almost right. Learning these traps in advance means you won't fall for them under pressure!

The 5 Classic GL Traps
  • 1. The 4/5 Rule Trap: An answer passes 4 out of 5 rules but fails one. It looks almost perfect. Only a full SPANS scan catches it.
  • 2. The Orientation Illusion: Same shape, rotated — students reject it thinking it's a different shape. Always mentally spin the shape before deciding.
  • 3. The Overcounting Trap: Students count internal + external elements together. They follow separate rules.
  • 4. The Visual Bias Trap: A larger or bolder shape distracts the eye. Students focus on the big obvious shape and miss a small rule (e.g., a tiny dot in the corner).
  • 5. The Mirror Trap: Correct elements, but mirrored (left↔right or top↔bottom). "Top-right corner" and "top-left corner" look similar under time pressure.
